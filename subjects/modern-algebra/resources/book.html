Part I · Foundations and Core Language

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

1 · Groups and group actions

Group structure separates internal subgroups from quotient information. Group actions represent abstract elements as transformations of a set.

1.1 · Group structure

Group structure separates internal subgroups from quotient information. Homomorphisms reveal which features survive a structure-preserving map.

1.1.1 · Subgroups and quotients

Subgroups inherit the group operation, while quotients identify elements modulo a normal subgroup. These constructions describe internal structure and controlled identifications.

1.1.2 · Normality

A subgroup is normal when it is preserved by conjugation. Normality is exactly the condition needed for the usual quotient group operation.

1.1.3 · Homomorphism theorems

The isomorphism theorems relate kernels, images, and quotients. They convert a homomorphism into a precise structural description.

1.2 · Actions

Group actions represent abstract elements as transformations of a set. Orbit counting turns these actions into information about the group.

1.2.1 · Orbits and stabilizers

Orbits collect points reachable by an action, and stabilizers fix a chosen point. The orbit-stabilizer formula relates their sizes in finite cases.

1.2.2 · Class equation

The class equation decomposes a finite group into conjugacy classes. It relates the center to centralizers and supports structural arguments.

1.2.3 · Permutation representations

A permutation representation sends group elements to permutations of a set. Faithful actions embed the group into a symmetric group.

2 · Finite group theory

Sylow theory locates the largest prime-power subgroups of a finite group. Structural classes organize groups by successive commutators and normal subgroups.

2.1 · Sylow theory

Sylow theory locates the largest prime-power subgroups of a finite group. Counting and conjugacy restrictions then constrain possible group structures.

2.1.1 · Sylow theorems

The Sylow theorems establish existence, conjugacy, and number restrictions for Sylow subgroups. They turn prime factorization of the group order into group-theoretic constraints.

2.1.2 · Conjugacy

Conjugate subgroups differ by an internal change of coordinates in the group. Sylow conjugacy explains why maximal prime-power subgroups share a structural role.

2.1.3 · Applications

Sylow counts can force normal subgroups and rule out simple groups of certain orders. Each argument uses both divisibility and congruence restrictions on the count.

2.2 · Structural classes

Structural classes organize groups by successive commutators and normal subgroups. Composition series isolate the simple factors beneath that organization.

2.2.1 · P-groups

A finite p-group has order a power of one prime. Its nontrivial center is a fundamental consequence of the class equation.

2.2.2 · Solvable and nilpotent groups

Solvable groups have terminating derived series, while nilpotent groups admit a stronger central-series condition. Comparing them clarifies different levels of noncommutativity.

2.2.3 · Composition series

A composition series has simple successive quotients. The Jordan–Hölder theorem makes its factors invariant up to order and isomorphism.

3 · Rings and ideals

Ring homomorphisms preserve addition, multiplication, and the chosen unit convention. Special integral domains support increasingly strong division and factorization principles.

3.1 · Ring homomorphisms

Ring homomorphisms preserve addition, multiplication, and the chosen unit convention. Ideals encode their kernels and quotient constructions.

3.1.1 · Ideals

Ideals are additive subgroups stable under multiplication by ring elements in the appropriate sided sense. They are the subsets used to form ring quotients.

3.1.2 · Quotient rings

A quotient ring identifies elements differing by an ideal. It encodes relations while preserving ring operations.

3.1.3 · Universal properties

Universal properties characterize maps into or out of a construction. They explain quotient rings independently of elementwise formulas.

3.2 · Special domains

Special integral domains support increasingly strong division and factorization principles. Examples distinguish implications from equivalences.

3.2.1 · Euclidean domains

A Euclidean domain has a size function supporting division with smaller remainder. The Euclidean algorithm then constructs greatest common divisors.

3.2.2 · PIDs

A principal ideal domain has every ideal generated by one element. This condition yields strong module and factorization theorems.

3.2.3 · UFDs and examples

A unique factorization domain has unique factorization into irreducibles up to units and order. It need not admit Euclidean division or have every ideal principal.

Part II · Central Theory and Techniques

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

4 · Modules and exact sequences

Modules generalize vector spaces by replacing the scalar field with a ring. Exact sequences express how maps fit together through kernels and images.

4.1 · Module basics

Modules generalize vector spaces by replacing the scalar field with a ring. Familiar linear constructions persist, but bases and division behave differently.

4.1.1 · Submodules and quotients

Submodules are subsets stable under the module operations. Quotient modules impose additive and scalar-compatible identifications.

4.1.2 · Cyclic modules

A cyclic module is generated by a single element. Its annihilator often presents it as a quotient of the ring.

4.1.3 · Free modules

A free module has a basis with unique finite linear expansions. General modules may fail to be free even when finitely generated.

4.2 · Exactness

Exact sequences express how maps fit together through kernels and images. Splitting and classification show when a module can be reconstructed from simpler pieces.

4.2.1 · Kernels and cokernels

A kernel records elements mapped to zero, while a cokernel quotients the target by the image. Together they measure injectivity and surjectivity failures.

4.2.2 · Splitting

A split short exact sequence identifies the middle module with a direct sum. The existence of a section or retraction certifies splitting.

4.2.3 · Structure over a PID

Finitely generated modules over a PID decompose into free and cyclic torsion parts. This unifies abelian-group classification and operator canonical forms.

5 · Polynomial and commutative algebra

Polynomial rings connect abstract ring theory to equations and extensions. Noetherian methods replace potentially infinite relations by finite generators.

5.1 · Polynomial rings

Polynomial rings connect abstract ring theory to equations and extensions. Content and primitivity control the relation between coefficient-ring and fraction-field factorization.

5.1.1 · Gauss lemma

Gauss's lemma makes products of primitive polynomials primitive over a UFD. It links irreducibility over the domain to irreducibility over its fraction field.

5.1.2 · Factorization

Polynomial factorization depends on the coefficient ring or field. Tracking that ambient structure prevents incorrect irreducibility claims.

5.1.3 · Content and primitive polynomials

Content is the common factor of coefficients up to units, and a primitive polynomial has unit content. Separating content isolates the genuinely polynomial part of factorization.

5.2 · Noetherian methods

Noetherian methods replace potentially infinite relations by finite generators. Localization then concentrates those relations near selected primes.

5.2.1 · Noetherian rings

A Noetherian ring satisfies the ascending-chain condition on ideals. Equivalently, every ideal is finitely generated.

5.2.2 · Hilbert basis theorem

Hilbert's basis theorem preserves Noetherianity when adjoining finitely many polynomial variables. It supplies a key finiteness property of algebraic geometry.

5.2.3 · Localization

Localization inverts a chosen multiplicative set. It separates local behavior from global ring structure.

6 · Field theory

Algebraic field extensions adjoin elements satisfying polynomial equations. Splitting fields provide domains containing all roots of chosen polynomials.

6.1 · Algebraic extensions

Algebraic field extensions adjoin elements satisfying polynomial equations. Degree and separability control the resulting linear and root structure.

6.1.1 · Minimal polynomials

The minimal polynomial is the monic irreducible polynomial of an algebraic element over the base field. It records all polynomial relations of that element.

6.1.2 · Degree

Extension degree is dimension as a vector space over the base field. It bounds and counts algebraic complexity in finite extensions.

6.1.3 · Towers and separability

Degrees multiply along finite towers, while separability concerns distinct roots of minimal polynomials. These properties govern how embeddings extend.

6.2 · Splitting fields

Splitting fields provide domains containing all roots of chosen polynomials. Normality and separability lead to the Galois setting.

6.2.1 · Normality

An algebraic extension is normal when irreducible base polynomials with one root in the extension split there. Splitting fields are the basic examples.

6.2.2 · Algebraic closure

An algebraic closure is an algebraically closed algebraic extension. It supplies a common ambient field for comparing finite extensions.

6.2.3 · Primitive element theorem

The primitive element theorem makes finite separable extensions simple. It allows several generators to be replaced by one under its hypotheses.

7 · Galois theory

Galois theory studies field extensions through their automorphism groups. Solvability by radicals turns root expressions into a group-theoretic question.

7.1 · Galois groups

Galois theory studies field extensions through their automorphism groups. Fixed fields translate subgroup structure back into intermediate fields.

7.1.1 · Automorphisms

A base-field automorphism preserves field operations while fixing the base. Such maps permute algebraic roots compatibly.

7.1.2 · Fixed fields

The fixed field of a subgroup consists of elements unchanged by every group element. It is the field-side counterpart of a symmetry constraint.

7.1.3 · Fundamental theorem

For finite Galois extensions, the fundamental theorem gives an inclusion-reversing correspondence between subgroups and intermediate fields. Normal subgroups identify intermediate Galois extensions.

7.2 · Solvability by radicals

Solvability by radicals turns root expressions into a group-theoretic question. Cyclotomic extensions provide important explicit examples.

7.2.1 · Solvable groups

A solvable group has a derived series ending at the identity. In characteristic zero, this property characterizes solvability by radicals for the relevant Galois group.

7.2.2 · Cyclotomic fields

Cyclotomic fields adjoin roots of unity. Their explicit Galois groups connect field theory to congruence arithmetic.

7.2.3 · Abel–Ruffini framework

Abel–Ruffini rules out a general radical formula for polynomial equations of degree at least five. The obstruction is the nonsolvability of appropriate Galois groups, not the impossibility of solving every individual equation.

8 · Tensor, categories, and constructions

Tensor products linearize balanced bilinear constructions over rings. Categorical language describes algebraic constructions through mapping behavior.

8.1 · Tensor products

Tensor products linearize balanced bilinear constructions over rings. They organize scalar changes and interactions between modules.

8.1.1 · Bilinear maps

A balanced bilinear map respects the module scalar actions as well as additivity. It factors through the corresponding tensor product.

8.1.2 · Extension of scalars

Extension of scalars tensors a module with a larger coefficient ring. It transports algebraic objects while retaining the specified base relation.

8.1.3 · Examples

Tensoring cyclic modules provides explicit calculations with generators and relations. These examples also show that tensoring need not preserve injective maps.

8.2 · Categorical language

Categorical language describes algebraic constructions through mapping behavior. It reveals common patterns behind superficially different objects.

8.2.1 · Products and coproducts

Products collect compatible projections, while coproducts collect compatible inclusions. Their forms depend on the algebraic category under consideration.

8.2.2 · Universal properties

A universal property specifies a unique factorization for every appropriate map. This determines a construction up to unique isomorphism.

8.2.3 · Adjunction examples

Tensor-Hom and extension-restriction adjunctions relate maps in different settings. They explain why common algebraic constructions come in pairs.

Part III · Further Theory and Applications

9 · Homological algebra introduction

Chain complexes encode sequences of maps with consecutive composite zero. Derived functors measure what ordinary functors fail to preserve in exact sequences.

9.1 · Chain complexes

Chain complexes encode sequences of maps with consecutive composite zero. Their homology measures failures of exactness.

9.1.1 · Cycles, boundaries, and homology

Cycles are killed by the next differential, while boundaries come from the previous one. Homology is their quotient and measures the surviving classes.

9.1.2 · Exact sequences

Exactness means that every cycle is a boundary at the relevant position. Exact sequences organize relations between algebraic objects.

9.1.3 · Resolutions

A resolution replaces a module by an exact complex of better-behaved modules. Projective or injective resolutions support derived constructions.

9.2 · Derived functors

Derived functors measure what ordinary functors fail to preserve in exact sequences. Ext and Tor are the principal examples introduced here.

9.2.1 · Ext and Tor motivation

Ext measures failures of Hom exactness, while Tor measures failures of tensor exactness. Their low-degree groups detect extension and torsion phenomena.

9.2.2 · Computation over familiar rings

Resolutions over integers and polynomial rings allow explicit derived-functor calculations. These examples connect abstract definitions to familiar modules.

9.2.3 · Applications

Derived functors detect splitting obstructions and influence base-change behavior. Their long exact sequences make related computations communicate.

10 · Selected advanced algebra

Finite fields and discrete valuations provide two important arithmetic settings for algebra. Synthesis compares the proof patterns behind major classification results.

10.1 · Finite fields and valuations

Finite fields and discrete valuations provide two important arithmetic settings for algebra. Both connect structural theory with explicit examples.

10.1.1 · Finite-field structure

Finite fields have prime-power order and cyclic multiplicative groups. Their subfields and automorphisms are controlled by the Frobenius map.

10.1.2 · Discrete valuations

A discrete valuation assigns integer orders to nonzero field elements with multiplicative additivity. Its valuation ring has a distinguished maximal ideal and local divisibility structure.

10.1.3 · Examples

Finite extensions of finite fields and valuations on rational-function fields make the definitions concrete. The examples highlight different ways algebra records arithmetic.

10.2 · Synthesis and further study

Synthesis compares the proof patterns behind major classification results. It also practices finding and repairing invalid uses of structural theorems.

10.2.1 · Structure theorems

Structure theorems decompose objects under specific hypotheses such as finite generation or principality. Their usefulness depends on checking those hypotheses before applying the classification.

10.2.2 · Proof repair

Proof repair identifies the first unsupported inference and the missing condition behind it. Counterexamples help distinguish a repairable statement from a false conclusion.

10.2.3 · Algebraic geometry and representation links

Algebraic geometry uses rings and modules as local coordinates, while representation theory uses modules to encode symmetry. Both reuse the structural and homological methods developed here.
