Part I · Foundations and Core Language

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

1 · Metric foundations for analysis

Metric spaces provide a common language for limits and continuity. Sequences of functions have several inequivalent convergence notions.

1.1 · Metric spaces

Metric spaces provide a common language for limits and continuity. Completeness, compactness, and countability determine which analytic arguments are available.

1.1.1 · Completeness

Completeness means that every Cauchy sequence has a limit in the space. It prevents approximation processes from leaving the setting.

1.1.2 · Compactness

Compactness is the finite-subcover property for open covers. In metric spaces, it is equivalent to sequential compactness.

1.1.3 · Separability

A separable space has a countable dense subset. This countability often reduces approximation arguments to manageable families.

1.2 · Sequences of functions

Sequences of functions have several inequivalent convergence notions. Their differences determine whether continuity or other properties survive a limit.

1.2.1 · Pointwise and uniform convergence

Pointwise convergence fixes the input before taking the limit, while uniform convergence controls every input at once. Uniform limits of continuous functions remain continuous.

1.2.2 · Equicontinuity

Equicontinuity gives a common continuity estimate for a family at each point. It supports subsequence compactness on compact domains.

1.2.3 · Arzelà–Ascoli preview

Arzelà–Ascoli turns equicontinuity and boundedness into compactness under suitable domain hypotheses. It previews how function-space existence proofs extract convergent subsequences.

2 · Measure and measurable sets

Outer measures construct size before all measurable sets have been identified. Measurability specifies which sets and maps belong to an integration theory.

2.1 · Outer measure

Outer measures construct size before all measurable sets have been identified. Carathéodory's criterion selects the sets on which additivity behaves correctly.

2.1.1 · Carathéodory construction

The Carathéodory criterion tests whether a set splits every subset additively in outer measure. The selected sets form a sigma-algebra supporting a measure.

2.1.2 · Lebesgue measure

Lebesgue measure extends Euclidean length and volume to a broad measurable class. Its translation invariance supports analysis on Euclidean space.

2.1.3 · Measurable completion

Completion adds all subsets of null sets to the measurable structure. This makes almost-everywhere modifications easier to handle.

2.2 · Measurable structure

Measurability specifies which sets and maps belong to an integration theory. Null sets allow conclusions that hold outside negligible exceptions.

2.2.1 · Borel sets

Borel sets form the sigma-algebra generated by open sets. They connect topology to measure theory.

2.2.2 · Measurable maps

A measurable map pulls measurable target sets back to measurable source sets. This condition ensures that compositions and distributions are well defined.

2.2.3 · Null sets and almost-everywhere statements

A null set has measure zero, and an almost-everywhere statement may fail only on such a set. Countable exceptional unions remain negligible.

3 · Lebesgue integration

The Lebesgue integral is built from simple functions. Riemann integration is recovered within the wider Lebesgue framework.

3.1 · Simple function approximation

The Lebesgue integral is built from simple functions. The construction emphasizes monotone approximation and control of absolute size.

3.1.1 · Definition of integral

Simple integrals weight measurable-set sizes by finitely many values. Suprema and positive-negative decomposition extend this definition to general measurable functions.

3.1.2 · Monotone approximation

Increasing simple functions approximate every nonnegative measurable function. This makes the integral compatible with monotone limits.

3.1.3 · Integrability criteria

Integrability requires finite integral of the absolute value. Comparison estimates give practical sufficient conditions for this property.

3.2 · Comparison with Riemann integration

Riemann integration is recovered within the wider Lebesgue framework. Comparing the theories explains why measure-theoretic integration handles limits more effectively.

3.2.1 · Darboux criterion

The Darboux criterion compares upper and lower sums over partitions. Their arbitrarily small difference characterizes Riemann integrability of bounded functions on intervals.

3.2.2 · Discontinuity sets

A bounded function on a compact interval is Riemann integrable exactly when its discontinuities have measure zero. This connects partition methods with measurable exceptional sets.

3.2.3 · Convergence under the integral

Moving limits through an integral requires hypotheses such as domination or monotonicity. Examples contrast valid interchanges with concentration effects that break them.

Part II · Central Theory and Techniques

4 · Convergence theorems and modes

Integral limit theorems provide precise ways to compare limits and integrals. Function convergence can be pointwise, measure-based, or norm-based.

4.1 · Integral limit theorems

Integral limit theorems provide precise ways to compare limits and integrals. Their distinct hypotheses address different approximation patterns.

4.1.1 · Monotone convergence

Monotone convergence applies to increasing nonnegative measurable functions. The integrals increase to the integral of the limit.

4.1.2 · Fatou lemma

Fatou's lemma gives a lower-semicontinuity inequality for nonnegative functions. It is useful when convergence is insufficient for equality.

4.1.3 · Dominated convergence

Dominated convergence applies when an integrable majorant bounds a pointwise almost-everywhere convergent sequence. It turns that convergence into convergence of integrals.

4.2 · Modes of convergence

Function convergence can be pointwise, measure-based, or norm-based. The section tracks implications and counterexamples between these modes.

4.2.1 · Almost everywhere

Almost-everywhere convergence means pointwise convergence outside a fixed null set. It does not by itself control integral size.

4.2.2 · In measure

Convergence in measure means that sets with error above any fixed threshold shrink in measure. On finite measure spaces, almost-everywhere convergence implies it.

4.2.3 · In Lp and in distribution

Lp convergence controls integrated error, while convergence in distribution tests induced probability laws. The latter belongs naturally to the probability-space specialization and is generally weaker.

5 · Lp spaces and inequalities

Lp norms turn integrability conditions into quantitative geometry. Dense classes and dual representations simplify analysis of general Lp functions.

5.1 · Norms and completeness

Lp norms turn integrability conditions into quantitative geometry. Hölder and Minkowski provide the fundamental estimates behind completeness.

5.1.1 · Hölder

Hölder bounds the integral of a product using conjugate-exponent norms. It supports dual pairings and integrability tests.

5.1.2 · Minkowski

Minkowski is the triangle inequality for Lp when p is at least one. It ensures that addition respects the normed-space structure.

5.1.3 · Completeness of Lp

Completeness of Lp guarantees limits of norm-Cauchy sequences. Its proof connects measure-theoretic convergence to functional analysis.

5.2 · Approximation and duality

Dense classes and dual representations simplify analysis of general Lp functions. Translation continuity supports approximation by smoothing.

5.2.1 · Simple-function density

Simple functions are dense in Lp for finite p. This reduces many arguments to finitely many measurable pieces.

5.2.2 · Translation continuity

Translations are continuous in the Lp norm on Euclidean space for finite p. This fact underlies convolution approximation and smoothing.

5.2.3 · Dual representations

Continuous linear functionals on many Lp spaces are represented by conjugate-exponent functions. Endpoint and measure-space assumptions must be stated separately.

6 · Product measures and Fubini theory

Product construction assigns measures to joint-variable spaces. Iterated integration separates a multidimensional problem into successive one-variable integrals.

6.1 · Product construction

Product construction assigns measures to joint-variable spaces. Rectangles provide the elementary data from which the joint theory is extended.

6.1.1 · Rectangle measures

A measurable rectangle receives the product of its factor measures. These assignments determine the standard product measure under usual sigma-finiteness assumptions.

6.1.2 · Product sigma-algebra

The product sigma-algebra is generated by rectangles with measurable factors. It makes coordinate projections and joint measurable functions accessible.

6.1.3 · Tonelli theorem

Tonelli equates nonnegative joint and iterated integrals. The conclusion allows infinite values without cancellation problems.

6.2 · Iterated integration

Iterated integration separates a multidimensional problem into successive one-variable integrals. Absolute integrability and measurability make that separation rigorous.

6.2.1 · Fubini theorem

Fubini equates absolutely integrable joint integrals with their iterated versions. It also ensures almost-everywhere integrability of slices.

6.2.2 · Parameterized integrals

Parameterized integrals treat one group of variables as data for another integration. Their measurability and continuity follow from appropriate integral theorems.

6.2.3 · Counterexamples to hypothesis removal

Conditionally convergent or nonmeasurable examples can defeat naive interchange. Studying them identifies the role of each hypothesis.

7 · Signed and complex measures

Signed and complex measures extend positive measures while retaining controlled variation. Radon–Nikodym theory expresses one measure using a density relative to another.

7.1 · Variation and decomposition

Signed and complex measures extend positive measures while retaining controlled variation. Decomposition separates cancellation from absolute size.

7.1.1 · Total variation

The total variation measure records the absolute size of a signed or complex measure. It controls integration and defines a natural norm for finite measures.

7.1.2 · Jordan decomposition

Jordan decomposition writes a signed measure as the difference of mutually singular positive measures under standard assumptions. It isolates positive and negative contributions.

7.1.3 · Signed integrals

Integration against a signed measure uses its positive and negative parts. Absolute integrability with respect to total variation avoids undefined cancellation.

7.2 · Radon–Nikodym theory

Radon–Nikodym theory expresses one measure using a density relative to another. Lebesgue decomposition separates the representable and singular parts.

7.2.1 · Absolute continuity

Absolute continuity of measures means preservation of null sets in one direction. Under suitable finiteness conditions it leads to density representation.

7.2.2 · Density theorem

Here the density theorem is the Radon–Nikodym representation by a measurable function. Its density is unique up to almost-everywhere equality.

7.2.3 · Lebesgue decomposition

Lebesgue decomposition splits a measure into an absolutely continuous part and a singular part. It clarifies which contributions can be captured by an ordinary density.

8 · Differentiation and covering methods

Covering and maximal-function methods control large local averages. Differentiation theorems recover local data from averages over shrinking regions.

8.1 · Maximal estimates

Covering and maximal-function methods control large local averages. They are the main tools behind differentiation of integrals.

8.1.1 · Covering lemmas

Covering lemmas select balls with controlled disjointness or overlap. These selections convert geometric local information into measure estimates.

8.1.2 · Weak bounds

Weak bounds estimate the measure of superlevel sets rather than a full norm. They are sufficient for many almost-everywhere conclusions.

8.1.3 · Maximal function

The maximal function takes the supremum of local absolute averages. It measures concentration uniformly over neighborhood sizes.

8.2 · Differentiation theorems

Differentiation theorems recover local data from averages over shrinking regions. Their conclusions are generally almost everywhere rather than everywhere.

8.2.1 · Differentiation of integrals

Lebesgue differentiation recovers a locally integrable function from local averages. It supplies the pointwise counterpart of the integral construction.

8.2.2 · Density theorem

Here the density theorem concerns measurable sets and shrinking-neighborhood proportions. Almost every point of a set has density one in that set.

8.2.3 · Applications

Differentiation yields approximate continuity and local recovery of measurable data. Applications must specify the underlying measure and averaging regions.

9 · Absolutely continuous and BV functions

Bounded variation controls accumulated oscillation on an interval. Absolute continuity is the regularity that restores integral recovery from a derivative.

9.1 · Variation

Bounded variation controls accumulated oscillation on an interval. It links real functions to signed measures and weak derivatives.

9.1.1 · Total variation

The variation of a function is the supremum of sums of absolute increments over partitions. It quantifies total oscillation without requiring classical differentiability.

9.1.2 · Functions of bounded variation

A function of bounded variation has finite total variation. On an interval it can be decomposed into a difference of monotone functions.

9.1.3 · Measures induced by monotone functions

Monotone functions determine Lebesgue–Stieltjes measures through their increments with a chosen continuity convention. This identifies jump and continuous contributions to variation.

9.2 · Fundamental theorem

Absolute continuity is the regularity that restores integral recovery from a derivative. It is stronger than continuity and excludes singular variation.

9.2.1 · Absolute continuity

Absolute continuity controls sums of function increments over disjoint intervals of small total length. It is the key hypothesis in the Lebesgue fundamental theorem.

9.2.2 · A.e. differentiability

Monotone and bounded-variation functions are differentiable almost everywhere. Such differentiability alone does not guarantee recovery by integrating the derivative.

9.2.3 · Integral representation

An absolutely continuous function equals an initial value plus the integral of its almost-everywhere derivative. This representation characterizes absolute continuity on a compact interval.

10 · Compactness, applications, and extensions

Egorov and Lusin connect measurable behavior with more regular behavior outside small exceptional sets. The final topics strengthen convergence tools and extend the target spaces of integration.

10.1 · Egorov and Lusin

Egorov and Lusin connect measurable behavior with more regular behavior outside small exceptional sets. Their measure hypotheses are part of their conclusions.

10.1.1 · Uniformity outside small sets

Egorov's theorem makes almost-everywhere convergence uniform off a set of arbitrarily small measure in the finite-measure setting. It localizes the failure of uniformity.

10.1.2 · Approximation by continuous functions

Lusin's theorem makes a measurable function continuous on a sufficiently large closed subset under standard Euclidean measure hypotheses. It approximates regularity through removal of a small set.

10.1.3 · Consequences

These theorems reduce measurable problems to controlled regular pieces. They complement density arguments and clarify the meaning of almost-everywhere assertions.

10.2 · Further directions

The final topics strengthen convergence tools and extend the target spaces of integration. They prepare connections to probability and partial differential equations.

10.2.1 · Uniform integrability

Uniform integrability prevents integral mass from concentrating in large tails. Combined with suitable convergence, it supports convergence of expectations or integrals.

10.2.2 · Banach-valued integration

Banach-valued integration requires measurability and norm-integrability conditions for vector-valued functions. It underlies many functional-analytic evolution problems.

10.2.3 · Probability and PDE connections

Probability interprets integrals as expectations, while PDE uses weak derivatives and function spaces. Both depend on the measure and convergence principles developed here.
