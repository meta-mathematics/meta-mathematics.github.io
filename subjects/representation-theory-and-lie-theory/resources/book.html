Part I · Foundations and Core Language

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

1 · Representations and modules

Representations realize abstract symmetries as linear transformations. Complete reducibility expresses a representation as a sum of irreducible pieces.

1.1 · Basic representations

Representations realize abstract symmetries as linear transformations. Their invariant subspaces and equivalences identify the essential linear action.

1.1.1 · Group actions on vector spaces

A representation assigns an invertible linear map to each group element compatibly with multiplication. This lets group questions be studied with linear algebra.

1.1.2 · Invariant subspaces

An invariant subspace is preserved by every operator in the representation. Restriction to it produces a smaller representation.

1.1.3 · Equivalence

Equivalent representations are related by an invertible intertwining map. They describe the same action after a change of linear coordinates.

1.2 · Complete reducibility

Complete reducibility expresses a representation as a sum of irreducible pieces. Field and group hypotheses determine when this decomposition exists.

1.2.1 · Maschke theorem

Maschke's theorem gives complete reducibility for finite groups when the field characteristic does not divide the group order. Averaging a projection supplies the proof mechanism.

1.2.2 · Schur lemma

Schur's lemma constrains intertwiners between irreducible representations. Over an algebraically closed field in finite dimensions, irreducible endomorphisms are scalar.

1.2.3 · Semisimple modules

A semisimple module is a direct sum of simple modules. This is the module-theoretic language for completely reducible representations.

2 · Characters of finite groups

Characters encode representations by traces of their operators. Character tables collect the irreducible trace data of a finite group.

2.1 · Character theory

Characters encode representations by traces of their operators. Over the complex numbers for finite groups, they determine decompositions efficiently.

2.1.1 · Traces

The trace of the representing matrix defines the character at a group element. It is invariant under change of basis and conjugation.

2.1.2 · Orthogonality relations

Irreducible complex characters are orthonormal for the normalized class-function inner product. These relations give multiplicity formulas.

2.1.3 · Regular character

The regular representation acts on functions or the group algebra by translation. Its character reveals that each irreducible occurs with multiplicity equal to its dimension.

2.2 · Character computations

Character tables collect the irreducible trace data of a finite group. Conjugacy and orthogonality constrain their entries.

2.2.1 · Conjugacy classes

Characters are constant on conjugacy classes. These classes therefore index the columns of a character table.

2.2.2 · Character tables

A character table lists irreducible characters against conjugacy classes. Orthogonality, dimensions, and known constructions help determine missing entries.

2.2.3 · Decomposition of representations

Inner products with irreducible characters recover decomposition multiplicities. This can avoid finding invariant subspaces directly.

3 · Induction, restriction, and products

Induction builds a group representation from a representation of a subgroup. Representations can be combined and transformed through standard linear constructions.

3.1 · Induced representations

Induction builds a group representation from a representation of a subgroup. Reciprocity relates this construction to restriction.

3.1.1 · Cosets and induced modules

Induced modules combine coset data with the original subgroup action. Tensoring over the subgroup algebra gives an intrinsic construction.

3.1.2 · Frobenius reciprocity

Frobenius reciprocity identifies suitable intertwiner spaces for induction and restriction. It turns one multiplicity problem into another.

3.1.3 · Examples

Induction from cyclic or small subgroups gives explicit representations of larger finite groups. These examples make the reciprocity formula concrete.

3.2 · Operations on representations

Representations can be combined and transformed through standard linear constructions. Their decompositions reveal additional structure in the symmetry.

3.2.1 · Tensor products

The tensor product uses the diagonal group action on pairs of factors. Its character is the product of the factor characters.

3.2.2 · Symmetric powers

Symmetric powers carry induced actions on symmetric tensors. They occur naturally in polynomial functions and invariant theory.

3.2.3 · Restriction and decomposition

Restriction forgets part of the acting group by passing to a subgroup. Decomposing the restricted action is the basic branching problem.

Part II · Central Theory and Techniques

4 · Representations of compact groups

Compactness permits invariant averaging through Haar measure. Peter–Weyl theory decomposes functions on compact groups using matrix coefficients.

4.1 · Invariant integration

Compactness permits invariant averaging through Haar measure. This replaces finite summation and produces invariant geometric structures.

4.1.1 · Haar measure motivation

Haar measure supplies translation-invariant integration on a compact group. Normalization gives total mass one and makes averaging possible.

4.1.2 · Averaging inner products

Averaging an inner product over a compact group produces an invariant positive inner product. Finite-dimensional continuous representations therefore become unitary.

4.1.3 · Complete reducibility

Orthogonal complements of invariant subspaces are invariant for unitary representations. This proves finite-dimensional complete reducibility for compact groups.

4.2 · Peter–Weyl theory

Peter–Weyl theory decomposes functions on compact groups using matrix coefficients. It generalizes Fourier series beyond abelian groups.

4.2.1 · Matrix coefficients

A matrix coefficient evaluates a representation between chosen vectors. These scalar functions retain the group's linear action data.

4.2.2 · Fourier decomposition

Irreducible unitary matrix coefficients give orthogonal components in the compact-group L2 space. Their density supports Fourier-type reconstruction.

4.2.3 · Compact-group examples

The circle recovers ordinary Fourier modes, while compact matrix groups yield higher-dimensional representations. Comparing them shows what changes beyond commutativity.

5 · Lie groups and Lie algebras

Lie groups combine a smooth manifold with compatible group operations. The Lie bracket records infinitesimal noncommutativity.

5.1 · Smooth groups

Lie groups combine a smooth manifold with compatible group operations. Differentiation converts their local symmetry into linear data.

5.1.1 · Matrix groups

Matrix Lie groups are smooth groups realized inside invertible matrices. They provide explicit examples and formulas for the general theory.

5.1.2 · Tangent space at identity

The tangent space at the identity carries the Lie algebra structure. Translation identifies tangent information at other group points with this space.

5.1.3 · Exponential map

The exponential map follows one-parameter subgroups from tangent vectors. It relates the Lie algebra to a neighborhood of the group identity.

5.2 · Lie algebra structure

The Lie bracket records infinitesimal noncommutativity. Adjoint actions and one-parameter subgroups connect this structure back to the group.

5.2.1 · Brackets

A Lie bracket is bilinear, antisymmetric, and satisfies the Jacobi identity. For matrix Lie algebras it is the commutator.

5.2.2 · Adjoint representation

The adjoint representation differentiates conjugation to an action on the Lie algebra. It links internal group symmetry to algebra automorphisms.

5.2.3 · Integration of one-parameter subgroups

Each Lie algebra element generates a one-parameter subgroup of a Lie group. This integrates a constant infinitesimal direction into global motion.

6 · Structure theory of Lie algebras

Solvable and nilpotent Lie algebras impose different restrictions on repeated brackets. Semisimple Lie algebras have no nonzero solvable ideals.

6.1 · Solvable and nilpotent algebras

Solvable and nilpotent Lie algebras impose different restrictions on repeated brackets. Their representations admit strong triangularity results under appropriate field hypotheses.

6.1.1 · Derived series

The derived series repeatedly takes brackets within the current algebra. Termination defines solvability.

6.1.2 · Engel and Lie theorems

Engel's theorem concerns nilpotent operator actions, while Lie's theorem triangularizes representations of solvable algebras over an algebraically closed characteristic-zero field. Their assumptions and conclusions should be kept distinct.

6.1.3 · Examples

Upper triangular and strictly upper triangular matrices illustrate solvable and nilpotent Lie algebras. Their bracket series make the distinction explicit.

6.2 · Semisimple algebras

Semisimple Lie algebras have no nonzero solvable ideals. In characteristic zero, invariant bilinear forms expose their structure.

6.2.1 · Killing form

The Killing form is the trace pairing of adjoint operators. Its degeneracy reflects structural properties of the Lie algebra.

6.2.2 · Cartan criteria

Cartan criteria characterize semisimplicity and solvability through trace conditions in characteristic zero. They turn bracket structure into linear-algebra tests.

6.2.3 · Decomposition principles

A semisimple Lie algebra decomposes into simple ideals over the standard characteristic-zero setting. This reduces classification to simple components.

7 · The sl2 model and highest weights

The Lie algebra sl2 provides the basic model for highest-weight arguments. Highest-weight modules extend the sl2 pattern to semisimple Lie algebras.

7.1 · sl2 representations

The Lie algebra sl2 provides the basic model for highest-weight arguments. Its finite-dimensional complex representations are controlled by elementary weight strings.

7.1.1 · Raising and lowering operators

Raising and lowering operators move eigenvectors between neighboring weights. Their commutator relations constrain the allowed string lengths.

7.1.2 · Weight strings

A weight string is a sequence of vectors linked by these operators. Finite-dimensionality forces the string to terminate at both ends.

7.1.3 · Classification

Finite-dimensional irreducible sl2 representations are indexed by nonnegative integer highest weights. Each such weight determines a representation with one more dimension than the weight.

7.2 · Highest-weight modules

Highest-weight modules extend the sl2 pattern to semisimple Lie algebras. Enveloping algebras and induced modules provide their algebraic construction.

7.2.1 · Universal enveloping algebra

The universal enveloping algebra converts Lie algebra representations into associative-algebra modules. Its defining relations preserve the original bracket.

7.2.2 · Verma modules

A Verma module is induced from a chosen highest-weight action of a Borel subalgebra. It is a universal starting point for highest-weight modules.

7.2.3 · Finite-dimensional quotients

Dominant integral highest weights produce finite-dimensional irreducible quotients in the complex semisimple setting. Other weights generally do not yield finite-dimensional modules.

8 · Root systems and Weyl groups

Cartan subalgebras provide simultaneous weight coordinates for semisimple structure. Root systems reduce the classification of complex semisimple Lie algebras to finite combinatorial data.

8.1 · Cartan subalgebras

Cartan subalgebras provide simultaneous weight coordinates for semisimple structure. Roots describe the adjoint action in those coordinates.

8.1.1 · Roots and weights

Roots are nonzero weights of the adjoint representation, while weights describe general representations. Both live in the dual of a Cartan subalgebra.

8.1.2 · Root spaces

Root spaces are eigenspaces for the Cartan action. Their brackets and dimensions constrain the algebra's structure.

8.1.3 · Reflections

Root reflections preserve the root system and generate the Weyl group. They encode symmetries of weights and chambers.

8.2 · Classification

Root systems reduce the classification of complex semisimple Lie algebras to finite combinatorial data. Dynkin diagrams display that data compactly.

8.2.1 · Dynkin diagrams

Dynkin diagrams record angles and relative lengths among simple roots. Connected diagrams classify complex simple Lie algebras.

8.2.2 · Weyl groups

The Weyl group is generated by reflections in roots. It acts on weight space and organizes character formulas.

8.2.3 · Simple Lie algebras

Complex simple Lie algebras fall into classical families and exceptional types. The classification rests on the correspondence with irreducible root systems.

9 · Representations of semisimple Lie algebras

Dominant integral weights label finite-dimensional irreducible representations of complex semisimple Lie algebras. Character formulas compute the weight multiplicities and dimensions of irreducible modules.

9.1 · Dominant weights

Dominant integral weights label finite-dimensional irreducible representations of complex semisimple Lie algebras. Their lattice structure also controls tensor-product questions.

9.1.1 · Weight lattices

The weight lattice consists of weights integral on the simple coroots. Its dominant chamber selects the usual highest-weight labels.

9.1.2 · Irreducible modules

Every finite-dimensional irreducible module has a highest weight in the dominant integral lattice. Conversely, each such weight determines one up to isomorphism.

9.1.3 · Tensor products

Tensor products decompose into irreducible highest-weight modules. Weight addition provides initial constraints, while multiplicities require further analysis.

9.2 · Character formulae

Character formulas compute the weight multiplicities and dimensions of irreducible modules. They turn root-system data into representation-theoretic information.

9.2.1 · Weyl character formula

The Weyl character formula expresses an irreducible character using alternating Weyl-group sums. Dominant integrality is part of its finite-dimensional setting.

9.2.2 · Dimensions

The Weyl dimension formula follows by evaluating the character appropriately at the identity. It computes dimensions from positive roots and the highest weight.

9.2.3 · Branching previews

Branching decomposes a representation after restriction to a subgroup or subalgebra. Highest weights and character identities provide tools for selected examples.

10 · Lie groups and global representation theory

A Lie algebra representation describes only infinitesimal symmetry. Concrete compact and noncompact groups illustrate the range of group representation theory.

10.1 · From algebra to group

A Lie algebra representation describes only infinitesimal symmetry. Passing to a chosen Lie group also requires attention to its global topology.

10.1.1 · Simply connected groups

Simply connected Lie groups eliminate the fundamental-group obstruction to integrating Lie algebra representations. They serve as universal group-level models for this step.

10.1.2 · Integration

A finite-dimensional Lie algebra representation integrates uniquely to the connected simply connected group with that Lie algebra. The integrated representation recovers the original one by differentiation.

10.1.3 · Global constraints

Descent to a quotient group requires trivial action of the relevant covering kernel. Thus groups sharing one Lie algebra can have different representation lists.

10.2 · Compact and noncompact examples

Concrete compact and noncompact groups illustrate the range of group representation theory. Unitarity and decomposition behave differently in these settings.

10.2.1 · Classical groups

General linear, orthogonal, symplectic, and related groups form the classical families. Their defining forms or determinant conditions reveal their basic symmetries.

10.2.2 · Unitary representations

A unitary representation preserves an inner product, possibly on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. This condition makes analytic methods available.

10.2.3 · Selected decomposition results

Compact groups have discrete irreducible decomposition in their regular representation. Noncompact settings can require continuous decompositions and additional hypotheses.

11 · Further directions and applications

Algebraic and geometric methods realize representations through varieties and sheaves. Applications use representation theory to organize equations and objects by symmetry.

11.1 · Algebraic and geometric representation theory

Algebraic and geometric methods realize representations through varieties and sheaves. These directions build on the structural Lie theory developed earlier.

11.1.1 · Algebraic groups

Algebraic groups carry group operations defined by algebraic morphisms. Their representations interact with both field characteristic and algebraic geometry.

11.1.2 · Flag varieties

Flag varieties parametrize nested subspaces or corresponding homogeneous structures. Their geometry reflects roots, Borel subgroups, and highest weights.

11.1.3 · Geometric ideas

Geometric constructions realize representations in spaces of sections or cohomology groups. They connect algebraic multiplicities with geometric invariants.

11.2 · Applications

Applications use representation theory to organize equations and objects by symmetry. The relevant representation must be matched to the mathematical model.

11.2.1 · Symmetry in geometry, mathematical physics, PDE, and combinatorics

Symmetry decompositions separate invariant components in geometric, physical, analytic, and combinatorial problems. They can simplify operators, count structures, or explain degeneracies through irreducible pieces.
