Part I · Foundations and Core Language

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

1 · Fourier series

Fourier series decompose periodic functions into trigonometric modes. Different summation procedures produce different convergence behavior.

1.1 · Orthogonal expansions

Fourier series decompose periodic functions into trigonometric modes. Orthogonality turns coefficients into coordinates in a Hilbert space.

1.1.1 · Trigonometric systems

Trigonometric functions form an orthogonal system on the circle with the usual measure. Their span is dense in the corresponding L2 space.

1.1.2 · Coefficients

Fourier coefficients are inner products with frequency modes. They measure how much of each oscillation is present.

1.1.3 · Parseval identity

Parseval's identity equates the L2 norm with the squared sum of Fourier coefficients. It expresses conservation of energy between physical and frequency descriptions.

1.2 · Convergence and summability

Different summation procedures produce different convergence behavior. This section distinguishes norm convergence from pointwise reconstruction.

1.2.1 · Pointwise and L2 convergence

L2 convergence follows from Hilbert-space completeness of the trigonometric system. Pointwise convergence requires separate hypotheses and arguments.

1.2.2 · Fejér kernels

Fejér kernels average Fourier partial sums using nonnegative kernels. They provide robust approximation for continuous periodic functions.

1.2.3 · Gibbs phenomenon

The Gibbs phenomenon is persistent overshoot near jumps in Fourier partial sums. It illustrates why pointwise convergence does not imply uniform accuracy.

2 · Fourier transform on the line

The Schwartz class consists of smooth functions with rapid decay of every derivative. Plancherel theory extends the Fourier transform beyond absolutely integrable functions.

2.1 · Schwartz class

The Schwartz class consists of smooth functions with rapid decay of every derivative. It is an especially stable setting for Fourier transformation.

2.1.1 · Test functions

Schwartz functions provide a test space for tempered distributions. Their regularity and decay justify many initial calculations.

2.1.2 · Decay

Rapid decay controls functions and derivatives after multiplication by arbitrary polynomial weights. It makes Fourier integrals and differentiation arguments well behaved.

2.1.3 · Inversion formula

Fourier inversion recovers a suitable function from its transform. Fixing normalization conventions is essential to the formula.

2.2 · L2 theory

Plancherel theory extends the Fourier transform beyond absolutely integrable functions. It identifies the transform as a unitary operation after normalization.

2.2.1 · Plancherel theorem

Plancherel's theorem preserves the L2 inner product under Fourier transformation. It creates a frequency representation for square-integrable functions.

2.2.2 · Extension by density

A bounded map on a dense subspace extends uniquely to the completion. This constructs the L2 transform from Schwartz functions.

2.2.3 · Uncertainty principles

Uncertainty principles prevent simultaneous concentration in position and frequency. They quantify the tradeoff inherent in localization.

3 · Convolution and approximation

Convolution averages one function against translated copies of another. Special kernels realize smoothing and boundary-extension processes.

3.1 · Convolution algebra

Convolution averages one function against translated copies of another. Its algebra is central to smoothing and translation-invariant operators.

3.1.1 · Fubini

Fubini justifies exchanging the integrals occurring in convolution identities. The required absolute-integrability conditions must be checked.

3.1.2 · Young inequality

Young's inequality bounds a convolution in an Lp space using norms of its factors. The exponent relation determines the admissible mapping property.

3.1.3 · Approximate identities

Approximate identities concentrate mass near the origin with controlled total size. Their convolutions recover functions in suitable convergence modes.

3.2 · Summability kernels

Special kernels realize smoothing and boundary-extension processes. Their limiting behavior connects analysis of operators with approximation.

3.2.1 · Approximation to identity

Approximation to the identity uses kernels converging toward point concentration. Density and translation continuity establish convergence of their convolutions.

3.2.2 · Poisson kernel

The Poisson kernel produces harmonic extensions and boundary approximations. It links Fourier multipliers with potential theory.

3.2.3 · Heat kernel

The heat kernel solves the heat equation by convolution. Its Gaussian scaling explains diffusion and smoothing.

Part II · Central Theory and Techniques

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

4 · Lp estimates and interpolation

Interpolation obtains intermediate bounds from endpoint information. Endpoint estimates frequently fail in ordinary strong Lp norms.

4.1 · Interpolation principles

Interpolation obtains intermediate bounds from endpoint information. It reduces the number of operator estimates that need direct proof.

4.1.1 · Convexity of operator bounds

Operator bounds often vary logarithmically convexly with reciprocal exponents. This geometry guides interpolation between known estimates.

4.1.2 · Riesz–Thorin theorem

Riesz–Thorin interpolates strong bounds for linear operators between Lp spaces. Its complex-analytic proof uses analytic families and the three-lines principle.

4.1.3 · Marcinkiewicz theorem

Marcinkiewicz interpolation converts suitable weak endpoint bounds into strong interior bounds for sublinear operators. It is essential for maximal and singular-integral estimates.

4.2 · Endpoint behavior

Endpoint estimates frequently fail in ordinary strong Lp norms. Distribution functions and refined spaces capture the remaining control.

4.2.1 · Weak-type estimates

A weak-type bound controls the measure of superlevel sets of an operator output. It can hold even when the corresponding strong norm estimate fails.

4.2.2 · Distribution functions

A distribution function records the measure of points where a function exceeds a given magnitude. Layer-cake formulas connect it to integral norms.

4.2.3 · Lorentz-space motivation

Lorentz spaces refine Lp spaces using rearrangements and distribution functions. They describe endpoint behavior more precisely.

5 · Maximal functions and differentiation

Covering arguments select controlled subfamilies of balls from complicated collections. Maximal inequalities control exceptional sets in local averaging limits.

5.1 · Covering lemmas

Covering arguments select controlled subfamilies of balls from complicated collections. They convert local averaging information into global measure bounds.

5.1.1 · Vitali and Besicovitch ideas

Vitali and Besicovitch covering principles organize overlaps among balls. Their geometric conclusions drive differentiation and maximal estimates.

5.1.2 · Maximal operator

The Hardy–Littlewood maximal operator takes the supremum of local absolute averages. It detects where a function has unusually large local mass.

5.1.3 · Weak (1,1) estimate

The weak (1,1) estimate bounds maximal-function superlevel measure by the L1 norm divided by the threshold. A covering lemma supplies the main argument.

5.2 · Differentiation consequences

Maximal inequalities control exceptional sets in local averaging limits. This produces differentiation and density conclusions almost everywhere.

5.2.1 · Lebesgue differentiation

Lebesgue differentiation recovers an integrable function from shrinking averages almost everywhere. Maximal estimates control the error after approximation by continuous functions.

5.2.2 · Density points

A density point is a point where a measurable set occupies asymptotically all of small neighborhoods. Almost every point of a measurable set has this property.

5.2.3 · Maximal inequalities

Maximal inequalities bound the size of exceptional sets uniformly over averaging scales. They are a standard route from dense subclasses to general functions.

6 · Singular integrals

Singular kernels require cancellation because their absolute size may not be integrable near the diagonal. Calderón–Zygmund theory combines kernel regularity with boundedness and decomposition arguments.

6.1 · Kernel conditions

Singular kernels require cancellation because their absolute size may not be integrable near the diagonal. Truncations define manageable approximations to the operator.

6.1.1 · Size and cancellation

Kernel size bounds control singular growth, while cancellation offsets that growth. Both are needed in standard singular-integral theories.

6.1.2 · Principal values

A principal value is a limit with a prescribed symmetric exclusion of the singularity. Existence must be proved rather than assumed.

6.1.3 · Truncations

Truncated operators remove a neighborhood of the singularity. Uniform estimates allow a limit operator to be defined and controlled.

6.2 · Calderón–Zygmund theory

Calderón–Zygmund theory combines kernel regularity with boundedness and decomposition arguments. It extends singular-integral estimates across a range of exponents.

6.2.1 · L2 estimates

L2 estimates give an initial boundedness result, often through Fourier methods. They serve as an anchor for wider Lp theory.

6.2.2 · Decomposition

The Calderón–Zygmund decomposition splits a function into controlled good and localized bad parts. Cancellation of the bad parts compensates for their large size.

6.2.3 · Lp boundedness

Lp boundedness follows from endpoint estimates and interpolation under the theorem's kernel assumptions. The permissible exponent range is part of the result.

7 · Littlewood–Paley methods

Dyadic frequency decomposition separates a function into scales. Littlewood–Paley estimates characterize function norms through frequency pieces.

7.1 · Frequency decompositions

Dyadic frequency decomposition separates a function into scales. It makes regularity and interaction between frequencies visible.

7.1.1 · Dyadic partitions

A dyadic partition divides frequency space into regions of geometrically increasing size. Smooth cutoffs keep the associated operators manageable.

7.1.2 · Projections

Frequency projections extract the pieces selected by a partition. Their almost-orthogonality supports reconstruction and norm estimates.

7.1.3 · Square functions

A square function aggregates the magnitudes of pieces quadratically. It measures the collective contribution of different scales.

7.2 · Function-space estimates

Littlewood–Paley estimates characterize function norms through frequency pieces. They provide tools for regularity and nonlinear product estimates.

7.2.1 · Square-function inequalities

Square-function inequalities compare a function's norm with the norm of its scale aggregate. They extend orthogonality ideas beyond L2.

7.2.2 · Sobolev characterization

Sobolev regularity can be expressed through weighted frequency-piece norms. This translates differentiability into quantitative scale decay.

7.2.3 · Paraproduct motivation

Paraproducts separate products into low-high and comparable-frequency interactions. They organize nonlinear estimates that ordinary multiplication obscures.

8 · Oscillatory and restriction estimates

Oscillatory integrals gain decay through cancellation in rapidly varying phases. Fourier restriction asks when a Fourier transform has a meaningful trace on a curved set.

8.1 · Oscillatory integrals

Oscillatory integrals gain decay through cancellation in rapidly varying phases. The geometry of stationary points determines the estimate.

8.1.1 · Van der Corput lemma

Van der Corput estimates use lower bounds on suitable phase derivatives. They provide elementary control of one-dimensional oscillatory integrals.

8.1.2 · Stationary phase

Stationary phase approximates integrals near nondegenerate critical points of the phase. It identifies leading asymptotic contributions and decay rates.

8.1.3 · Decay estimates

Decay estimates quantify cancellation as an oscillation parameter grows. Their rate depends on phase geometry and amplitude regularity.

8.2 · Restriction phenomena

Fourier restriction asks when a Fourier transform has a meaningful trace on a curved set. Curvature and integrability determine what bounds can hold.

8.2.1 · Fourier restriction problem

The restriction problem seeks estimates for transforms restricted to lower-dimensional surfaces. Such traces are not defined for arbitrary Lp transforms without additional control.

8.2.2 · Maximal estimates

Maximal estimates control a supremum over a family of Fourier-related operators. They often support almost-everywhere convergence and restriction applications.

8.2.3 · Selected applications

Restriction estimates feed dispersive PDE bounds and geometric measure problems. Each application must match dimensions, exponents, and curvature hypotheses.

Part III · Further Theory and Applications

9 · Harmonic analysis on groups

Compact groups generalize periodic domains as spaces for harmonic decomposition. Locally compact groups support convolution and representations through Haar integration.

9.1 · Compact groups

Compact groups generalize periodic domains as spaces for harmonic decomposition. Haar measure and representation theory replace ordinary trigonometric coordinates.

9.1.1 · Haar measure

Haar measure is translation invariant and can be normalized to total mass one on a compact group. It defines the integrals used in group Fourier analysis.

9.1.2 · Characters

Characters are traces of representations and encode conjugacy-invariant information. For compact abelian groups, one-dimensional characters play the role of Fourier modes.

9.1.3 · Peter–Weyl theorem

Peter–Weyl gives density and orthogonality properties of finite-dimensional unitary matrix coefficients. It generalizes Fourier expansion on compact groups.

9.2 · Locally compact groups

Locally compact groups support convolution and representations through Haar integration. The abelian case admits a particularly direct duality theory.

9.2.1 · Convolution and representations

Convolution combines group functions using multiplication and Haar measure. Representations turn these convolutions into operator products.

9.2.2 · Abelian duality

Pontryagin duality relates a locally compact abelian group to its character group. It extends Fourier analysis beyond Euclidean and periodic settings.

9.2.3 · Examples

The line, the circle, discrete groups, and finite groups provide complementary examples. Comparing them reveals the common transform architecture.

10 · Applications and advanced directions

Harmonic estimates translate differential operators into frequency-space problems. Further topics refine localization in scale, position, and frequency.

10.1 · PDE applications

Harmonic estimates translate differential operators into frequency-space problems. This gives tools for propagation and regularity in PDE.

10.1.1 · Dispersive estimates

Dispersive estimates quantify spreading and decay of oscillatory evolution. They are building blocks for space-time estimates of solutions.

10.1.2 · Elliptic regularity

Elliptic regularity controls derivatives through elliptic equations and their data. Fourier methods provide transparent proofs in constant-coefficient model cases.

10.1.3 · Multiplier methods

Multiplier methods study operators that multiply Fourier transforms by a symbol. Symbol conditions determine their mapping properties on function spaces.

10.2 · Further topics

Further topics refine localization in scale, position, and frequency. They connect the core theory to data representations and difficult geometric questions.

10.2.1 · Wavelets

Wavelets use translated and dilated building blocks to represent functions. They combine scale information with spatial localization.

10.2.2 · Time-frequency analysis

Time-frequency analysis studies simultaneous localization in both variables. It is useful when a single global frequency decomposition is too coarse.

10.2.3 · Analytic number theory or fractal restriction

Analytic number theory and fractal restriction use oscillation under arithmetic or geometric constraints. These routes require specialized estimates beyond the core course.
