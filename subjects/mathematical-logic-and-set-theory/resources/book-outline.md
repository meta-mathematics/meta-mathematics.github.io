# Graduate Mathematical Logic and Set Theory: Graduate Course and Book Outline

> This is an original, graduate-level syllabus map synthesized from standard course coverage. It is a planning document, not a transcription of any one textbook table of contents. Topic order and depth vary by program and instructor.

## Audience and prerequisites

Proof-writing, discrete mathematics, and basic set theory; some courses assume algebra or computability.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of the course, a student should be able to:
- state the central definitions and explain the role of their hypotheses;
- prove and apply the core theorems in the syllabus, identifying which results are imported;
- construct examples and counterexamples that test the scope of a claim;
- connect the subject to at least one neighboring area without treating an analogy as a proof.

## Proposed chapter sequence

1. **Formal languages and proof systems.** Syntax, derivations, semantics, soundness, completeness, and formalization.
2. **First-order logic.** Structures, satisfaction, logical consequence, compactness, and Löwenheim–Skolem.
3. **Model theory.** Types, elementary extensions, quantifier elimination, saturation, and examples.
4. **Computability.** Turing machines, recursive and recursively enumerable sets, undecidability, and reductions.
5. **Gödel theorems.** Representability, incompleteness, consistency limitations, and scope of the theorems.
6. **Proof theory.** Natural deduction, sequent calculus, cut elimination, and consistency methods.
7. **Axiomatic set theory.** ZF/ZFC axioms, ordinals, transfinite recursion, and well-ordering.
8. **Cardinal arithmetic.** Cardinals, cofinality, regular and singular cardinals, and continuum questions.
9. **Choice and independence.** Axiom of choice, equivalent forms, constructibility, and independence results.
10. **Forcing.** Generic extensions, forcing relation, preservation, and a first independence application.
11. **Alternative foundations.** Constructive logic, type theory, and categorical foundations as comparative modules.
12. **Research practice.** Formal statement auditing, model construction, independence maps, and dependency tracking.

## Resource-collection plan

For each chapter, collect a primary textbook treatment, one alternative exposition or lecture-note source, prerequisite reminders, a representative theorem with a complete proof, examples and counterexamples, and exercises grouped by technique. Record exact editions, chapter or section numbers, stable URLs or catalog metadata, and any access or license restrictions. Keep source excerpts separate from original Meta-Math exposition.

## Future Meta-Math project pathway

Select projects only after the resource index is in place. Prefer a progression from a foundational theorem to a standard application and then to a graduate-level synthesis. For each selected project, record the exact statement, definitions, hypotheses, supporting imports, a dependency workflow, component-specific node activities, a continuous proof, a boundary test, and an evidence/review status. Avoid implying formal verification unless a proof assistant actually checks the stated theorem.

### Scope notes

Treat this syllabus as a flexible map. An instructor may omit or replace advanced units, and some programs teach a topic as a separate course. Before building a book index, note which chapters are core, optional, prerequisites, or deferred; cross-link overlapping material rather than duplicating it.
