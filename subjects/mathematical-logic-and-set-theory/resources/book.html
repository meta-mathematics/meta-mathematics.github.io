Part I · Foundations and Core Language

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

1 · Formal languages and deductive systems

Formal syntax specifies which symbol strings count as mathematical expressions. A proof system specifies permitted inferential steps.

1.1 · Syntax

Formal syntax specifies which symbol strings count as mathematical expressions. Precise formation rules make proofs and substitutions objects that can themselves be studied.

1.1.1 · Terms and formulas

Terms name objects, while formulas express relations and assertions about them. Inductive formation rules define both classes.

1.1.2 · Free and bound variables

A variable occurrence is bound when governed by a quantifier and free otherwise. This distinction determines what a formula depends on.

1.1.3 · Substitution

Substitution replaces free variables by terms while avoiding unintended variable capture. Its formal rules are needed for sound inference and coding arguments.

1.2 · Proof systems

A proof system specifies permitted inferential steps. Soundness connects those syntactic steps to semantic truth.

1.2.1 · Natural deduction

Natural deduction uses introduction and elimination rules for logical connectives and quantifiers. It closely reflects ordinary structured proof reasoning.

1.2.2 · Sequent calculus

Sequent calculus represents entailments with antecedent and consequent collections. Its rule structure is suited to structural and cut-elimination arguments.

1.2.3 · Soundness

Soundness means that derivable consequences are semantically valid in the intended class of structures. It is proved by checking the inference rules.

2 · First-order semantics

First-order semantics interprets formulas in mathematical structures. Completeness makes semantic consequence accessible through formal deduction.

2.1 · Structures and satisfaction

First-order semantics interprets formulas in mathematical structures. Satisfaction makes truth depend on the structure and any assignment to free variables.

2.1.1 · Interpretations

An interpretation assigns a domain and meanings to relation, function, and constant symbols. This turns formal language into a mathematical structure.

2.1.2 · Truth

Truth is defined recursively from atomic formulas through connectives and quantifiers. Sentences have truth values without free-variable assignments.

2.1.3 · Elementary equivalence

Elementarily equivalent structures satisfy the same first-order sentences. They can still differ in size or be nonisomorphic.

2.2 · Completeness and compactness

Completeness makes semantic consequence accessible through formal deduction. Compactness and Löwenheim–Skolem expose its consequences for model construction.

2.2.1 · Completeness theorem

First-order completeness states that every semantic consequence has a formal proof in a sound complete calculus. It concerns consequence, not the completeness of every axiomatic theory.

2.2.2 · Compactness theorem

Compactness says that a set of first-order sentences has a model if every finite subset does. It turns finite consistency checks into a global existence result.

2.2.3 · Löwenheim–Skolem

Löwenheim–Skolem theorems control model sizes relative to language cardinality. They reveal limits on first-order characterization of infinite structures.

3 · Model theory

Model theory studies theories through the structures satisfying them. Saturation and homogeneity concern realization of types and extension of partial symmetries.

3.1 · Types and theories

Model theory studies theories through the structures satisfying them. Types describe possible configurations of elements within those structures.

3.1.1 · Complete theories

A complete consistent theory decides every sentence in its language. This differs from a logic having a complete proof calculus.

3.1.2 · Types

A type is a consistent collection of formulas describing a potential tuple over specified parameters. Realization of types measures how rich a model is.

3.1.3 · Omitting types

Omitting-types theorems construct models that avoid selected nonprincipal types under countability hypotheses. They show that consistent descriptions need not occur in every model.

3.2 · Saturated and homogeneous models

Saturation and homogeneity concern realization of types and extension of partial symmetries. They help construct and compare models systematically.

3.2.1 · Elementary extensions

An elementary extension preserves truth of all formulas with parameters from the smaller structure. It enlarges the model without changing its first-order facts about existing elements.

3.2.2 · Back-and-forth

Back-and-forth constructions extend compatible finite or small partial maps alternately in both directions. Under suitable hypotheses they produce isomorphisms.

3.2.3 · Categoricity

Categoricity means uniqueness up to isomorphism among models of a specified cardinality. The cardinality and language setting are part of the claim.

Part II · Central Theory and Techniques

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

4 · Computability theory

Computability theory formalizes what an effective procedure can calculate. Undecidability identifies problems beyond any algorithm in the chosen model.

4.1 · Turing computation

Computability theory formalizes what an effective procedure can calculate. Equivalent computational models make the notion robust.

4.1.1 · Machines

A Turing machine performs local symbol operations according to finitely many rules. It provides a precise model of algorithmic computation.

4.1.2 · Recursive functions

Recursive-function formalisms define computable functions through basic functions and closure operations. Their equivalence with machine models links several views of effectiveness.

4.1.3 · Universal programs

A universal program simulates other encoded programs. It enables self-reference and diagonal undecidability arguments.

4.2 · Undecidability

Undecidability identifies problems beyond any algorithm in the chosen model. Reductions organize these limitations into reusable comparisons.

4.2.1 · Halting problem

The halting problem asks whether a coded program eventually stops on a coded input. No algorithm decides this for all program-input pairs.

4.2.2 · Many-one reductions

A many-one reduction transforms instances computably while preserving yes-or-no membership. It transfers undecidability from one problem to another.

4.2.3 · Recursively enumerable sets

A recursively enumerable set can have its members effectively listed or semidecided. Its complement need not be recursively enumerable.

5 · Incompleteness and formal arithmetic

Formal arithmetic can represent basic computations and encode syntax numerically. Incompleteness limits effectively axiomatized theories strong enough to express arithmetic.

5.1 · Representability

Formal arithmetic can represent basic computations and encode syntax numerically. These capabilities let a theory make statements about its own proofs.

5.1.1 · Coding syntax

Gödel coding assigns natural numbers to expressions and finite proof sequences. It turns syntactic relations into arithmetic predicates.

5.1.2 · Primitive recursive relations

Primitive recursive relations describe computations built by bounded structural recursion in the usual formalism. Their arithmetical representability supports proof coding.

5.1.3 · Derivability conditions

Derivability conditions formalize how an internal provability predicate behaves. They are central hypotheses in standard proofs of the second incompleteness theorem.

5.2 · Gödel theorems

Incompleteness limits effectively axiomatized theories strong enough to express arithmetic. The assumptions determine exactly which limitation follows.

5.2.1 · First and second incompleteness

The first theorem yields undecidable sentences for suitable consistent theories, and the second restricts internal proofs of their own consistency. Both concern sufficiently strong effective theories with appropriate formalizations.

5.2.2 · Consistency assumptions

Consistency and stronger soundness assumptions have distinct roles in different formulations. The selected theorem statement must say which assumption is actually used.

5.2.3 · Scope

Incompleteness does not say that every mathematical statement is undecidable or that all theories are incomplete. Its scope is limited by effectiveness, strength, and consistency hypotheses.

6 · Proof theory

Proof normalization removes redundant detours from formal deductions. Ordinal methods measure the complexity of proof reductions and induction principles.

6.1 · Normalization and cut elimination

Proof normalization removes redundant detours from formal deductions. Cut elimination gives a related structural simplification in sequent systems.

6.1.1 · Natural deduction normalization

Normalization transforms suitable natural-deduction proofs into normal forms without certain introduction-elimination detours. The result depends on the chosen logical system.

6.1.2 · Sequent calculus

Sequent proofs display antecedents, conclusions, and structural rules explicitly. This organization makes proof transformations precise.

6.1.3 · Cut elimination

Cut elimination removes applications of a rule composing proofs through an intermediate formula in systems where the theorem holds. It yields structural consequences such as subformula properties in appropriate formulations.

6.2 · Ordinal methods

Ordinal methods measure the complexity of proof reductions and induction principles. They compare the strength of formal arithmetic systems.

6.2.1 · Consistency proofs

A consistency proof rules out a derivation of contradiction relative to an ambient reasoning framework. Its assumptions must be stated when interpreting the conclusion.

6.2.2 · Proof-theoretic ordinals

A proof-theoretic ordinal records an induction or well-ordering strength associated with a theory in a specified analysis. It is a calibrated invariant rather than an arbitrary rank.

6.2.3 · Limits of formalization

Formal systems may be unable to internalize parts of their own metatheory. Incompleteness explains important limits on internal consistency arguments.

7 · Axiomatic set theory

Axiomatic set theory specifies the set-forming principles used as a mathematical foundation. Ordinals model well-ordered stages of iteration.

7.1 · ZF and ZFC

Axiomatic set theory specifies the set-forming principles used as a mathematical foundation. ZFC adds the axiom of choice to ZF.

7.1.1 · Extensionality and foundation

Extensionality identifies sets with the same members, while foundation restricts membership descent. These axioms govern identity and the shape of the set universe.

7.1.2 · Replacement

Replacement asserts that the image of a set under a definable functional relation is a set. It supports transfinite constructions beyond elementary separation.

7.1.3 · Choice

Choice selects one element from each member of a family of nonempty sets. Its equivalent forms support many algebraic and topological existence arguments.

7.2 · Ordinals and recursion

Ordinals model well-ordered stages of iteration. Transfinite recursion extends ordinary recursive construction beyond finite indices.

7.2.1 · Ordinal arithmetic

Ordinal arithmetic describes sums, products, and exponentiation of well-orders. It differs from cardinal arithmetic and is generally noncommutative.

7.2.2 · Transfinite recursion

Transfinite recursion defines each stage using the already constructed earlier stages. It is fundamental to cumulative hierarchies and ordinal-indexed constructions.

7.2.3 · Well-orders

A well-order gives every nonempty subset a least element. This supports transfinite induction and identifies an order type represented by an ordinal.

8 · Cardinals and combinatorics

Cardinals compare sizes of sets independently of their ordering. Infinite combinatorics studies structured configurations within large sets and orders.

8.1 · Cardinal arithmetic

Cardinals compare sizes of sets independently of their ordering. Cofinality distinguishes different ways infinite sizes can be approached.

8.1.1 · Cardinal operations

Cardinal addition, multiplication, and exponentiation represent disjoint union, Cartesian product, and function sets. Their laws depend on whether choice is assumed.

8.1.2 · Cofinality

Cofinality is the least size of an unbounded subset of an ordinal. It measures how many stages are needed to approach that ordinal.

8.1.3 · Regular and singular cardinals

An infinite cardinal is regular when its cofinality equals itself and singular otherwise. This distinction strongly influences infinite combinatorics.

8.2 · Combinatorial principles

Infinite combinatorics studies structured configurations within large sets and orders. Trees, stationary sets, and partition relations provide its basic languages.

8.2.1 · Trees

A set-theoretic tree is a partially ordered set whose predecessor sets are well ordered. Branches and levels encode combinatorial possibilities and obstructions.

8.2.2 · Stationary sets

A stationary subset of a regular uncountable cardinal meets every closed unbounded subset. It captures a robust form of largeness beyond unboundedness.

8.2.3 · Partition relations

Partition relations ask whether colorings admit large homogeneous subsets of prescribed type. They generalize finite Ramsey questions to infinite cardinals.

Part III · Further Theory and Applications

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

9 · Constructibility and independence

The constructible universe organizes sets by iterated definability. Independence means that an axiom system decides neither a statement nor its negation under stated consistency assumptions.

9.1 · Constructible universe

The constructible universe organizes sets by iterated definability. It is a canonical inner-model setting for relative-consistency arguments.

9.1.1 · L and relative consistency

The universe L is built by taking definable subsets stage by stage along the ordinals. Its internal properties yield important relative-consistency results for choice and the continuum hypothesis.

9.1.2 · Condensation overview

Condensation describes how suitable elementary pieces of levels of L collapse to other constructible levels. It reveals the hierarchy's fine structural regularity.

9.2 · Independence results

Independence means that an axiom system decides neither a statement nor its negation under stated consistency assumptions. Inner models and forcing provide complementary directions.

9.2.1 · Continuum hypothesis

The continuum hypothesis fixes the size of the continuum relative to the first uncountable cardinal. Its independence from ZFC is interpreted through relative-consistency results.

9.2.2 · Choice

The axiom of choice is independent of ZF in the standard relative-consistency sense. Models with and without choice illustrate its separate foundational role.

9.2.3 · Interpretation of relative consistency

A relative-consistency statement says that consistency of one theory implies consistency of another. It does not establish absolute consistency of either theory.

10 · Forcing and advanced foundations

Forcing constructs extensions of suitable ground models using partially ordered approximations. Advanced foundations compare model-building methods with alternative formal languages.

10.1 · Forcing basics

Forcing constructs extensions of suitable ground models using partially ordered approximations. Names and generic filters define the objects appearing in those extensions.

10.1.1 · Partial orders

Forcing conditions represent partial information ordered by strength. The partial order determines what the extension is designed to add or preserve.

10.1.2 · Names

Forcing names are recursively built instructions for objects in the extension. A generic filter interprets those instructions as actual sets.

10.1.3 · Generic extensions

A generic filter meets the dense sets required by the chosen ground-model setup. Its extension satisfies the forcing theorem and preserves specified axioms under the standard construction.

10.2 · Applications and alternatives

Advanced foundations compare model-building methods with alternative formal languages. The final route should keep mathematical results distinct from philosophical interpretations.

10.2.1 · Independence proofs

An independence proof constructs or interprets models with opposing truth values for a statement. It must record the background consistency assumptions and preservation arguments.

10.2.2 · Type theory

Type theory organizes expressions by types and formation rules rather than a single unrestricted membership relation. Different type theories offer different logical and computational foundations.

10.2.3 · Constructive or categorical foundations

Constructive and categorical foundations emphasize proof content or structural relationships in different ways. Their exact axioms must be specified before comparing their strength with set theory.
