Part I · Foundations and Core Language

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

1 · Holomorphic functions and power series

Complex differentiability imposes strong compatibility between two real variables. Power series give local representations with controlled convergence.

1.1 · Complex differentiability

Complex differentiability imposes strong compatibility between two real variables. This rigidity distinguishes holomorphic functions from general smooth planar maps.

1.1.1 · Cauchy–Riemann equations

The Cauchy–Riemann equations relate partial derivatives of real and imaginary parts. With suitable differentiability assumptions, they characterize holomorphicity.

1.1.2 · Analytic functions

Analytic functions are locally represented by convergent power series. Cauchy theory will show that holomorphic functions have this property.

1.1.3 · Elementary examples

Polynomials, exponentials, and local logarithms provide basic holomorphic examples. Their domains illustrate why singularities and branch choices matter.

1.2 · Power series

Power series give local representations with controlled convergence. They connect algebraic coefficients to analytic properties of functions.

1.2.1 · Radius of convergence

The radius of convergence separates absolute convergence inside a disk from divergence outside it. Boundary behavior requires a separate argument.

1.2.2 · Termwise operations

Power series can be differentiated and integrated term by term inside their convergence disks. Locally uniform convergence supplies the needed justification.

1.2.3 · Zeros and identity theorem

The first nonzero coefficient determines the order of a zero. Consequently, zeros accumulating inside a connected domain force the function to vanish identically.

2 · Cauchy theory

Contour integration integrates complex functions along oriented paths. Cauchy's theorems connect holomorphicity to contour integrals.

2.1 · Contour integration

Contour integration integrates complex functions along oriented paths. It links local differentiation to global properties of a domain.

2.1.1 · Paths and orientation

An oriented path supplies a parametrization and traversal direction for an integral. Reversing orientation changes the integral's sign.

2.1.2 · Primitives

A primitive is a holomorphic antiderivative on the chosen domain. Its existence makes integrals depend only on endpoints.

2.1.3 · Homotopy invariance

Integrals of a holomorphic function are unchanged under suitable endpoint-preserving homotopies. This makes domain topology relevant to integration.

2.2 · Cauchy theorems

Cauchy's theorems connect holomorphicity to contour integrals. Their consequences include analytic expansions and strong derivative bounds.

2.2.1 · Integral theorem

The integral theorem makes integrals vanish around appropriate closed contours in a holomorphic domain. The domain and contour hypotheses prevent hidden singularities.

2.2.2 · Integral formula

The integral formula recovers interior values from boundary integrals. Differentiating it gives formulas for every derivative.

2.2.3 · Derivative estimates

Cauchy estimates bound derivatives by boundary size and distance. They turn integral representation into compactness and rigidity results.

3 · Zeros and singularities

Holomorphic zeros have a discrete local structure unless the function vanishes identically. Laurent expansions describe holomorphic functions on annuli.

3.1 · Zero structure

Holomorphic zeros have a discrete local structure unless the function vanishes identically. Multiplicity makes this structure quantitative.

3.1.1 · Isolated zeros

A nontrivial holomorphic function has isolated zeros in a connected domain. Local power-series factorization explains this isolation.

3.1.2 · Multiplicity

Multiplicity is the order of the first nonzero term at a zero. It determines how zeros contribute to contour counts.

3.1.3 · Identity principle

The identity principle extends equality from an accumulating set to a connected domain. It supports uniqueness and analytic continuation.

3.2 · Laurent theory

Laurent expansions describe holomorphic functions on annuli. Negative powers distinguish different kinds of isolated singularities.

3.2.1 · Classification of isolated singularities

An isolated singularity is removable, a pole, or essential according to its Laurent principal part. The classification determines possible extensions and growth.

3.2.2 · Residues

The residue is the coefficient of the reciprocal first power in a Laurent expansion. It is exactly the local contribution to a contour integral.

3.2.3 · Essential singularities

An essential singularity has infinitely many negative Laurent coefficients. Nearby value behavior is much less restricted than at poles.

Part II · Central Theory and Techniques

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

4 · Residues and argument principles

The residue theorem expresses a contour integral through enclosed singularities. Logarithmic derivatives translate zeros and poles into integer contour counts.

4.1 · Residue theorem

The residue theorem expresses a contour integral through enclosed singularities. It is a local-to-global tool for explicit integration.

4.1.1 · Computing residues

Residues can be read from expansions or calculated with formulas for poles. Choosing an efficient method simplifies contour computations.

4.1.2 · Contour deformation

Contours may be deformed while preserving their relation to singularities. The residue theorem tracks changes when poles are crossed.

4.1.3 · Poles at infinity

The substitution by reciprocal coordinates studies a meromorphic function near infinity. It places poles and residues on the Riemann sphere.

4.2 · Zero and pole counting

Logarithmic derivatives translate zeros and poles into integer contour counts. This creates practical tests that avoid solving equations explicitly.

4.2.1 · Argument principle

The argument principle counts zeros minus poles using a logarithmic derivative integral. It requires a contour free of zeros and poles.

4.2.2 · Rouché theorem

Rouché's theorem preserves a zero count under a strict boundary comparison. It is useful when a complicated function is close to a simpler one.

4.2.3 · Applications to polynomial roots

Contours can isolate regions containing prescribed numbers of polynomial roots. Such counts complement explicit algebraic formulas and numerical guesses.

5 · Conformal mapping

Möbius maps are fractional linear transformations of the Riemann sphere. Schwarz-type arguments bound holomorphic maps by their domain geometry.

5.1 · Möbius transformations

Möbius maps are fractional linear transformations of the Riemann sphere. They organize conformal geometry through circles and normalization.

5.1.1 · Cross-ratios

The cross-ratio is an invariant of four points under Möbius transformations. It helps determine the unique map carrying three chosen points to another triple.

5.1.2 · Mapping disks and half-planes

Explicit fractional linear maps carry disks to half-planes. These models transfer analytic problems between standard domains.

5.1.3 · Normalization

Fixing images of selected points removes the freedom in a family of transformations. Normalization is essential in uniqueness statements.

5.2 · Schwarz lemmas

Schwarz-type arguments bound holomorphic maps by their domain geometry. Reflection and boundary considerations extend their practical reach.

5.2.1 · Schwarz lemma

Schwarz's lemma bounds a disk self-map fixing the origin and its derivative. Equality forces a rigid rotation under the standard hypotheses.

5.2.2 · Reflection

The Schwarz reflection principle extends functions across suitable real-analytic boundaries. Its hypotheses encode compatibility of boundary values.

5.2.3 · Boundary behavior

Boundary limits need not follow from interior holomorphicity alone. This subsection identifies additional domain and function assumptions that make them meaningful.

6 · Harmonic functions

Harmonic functions solve the planar Laplace equation and arise as parts of holomorphic functions. Poisson representation constructs harmonic functions from boundary data.

6.1 · Harmonicity and conjugates

Harmonic functions solve the planar Laplace equation and arise as parts of holomorphic functions. Their local properties connect complex analysis to potential theory.

6.1.1 · Laplace equation

The Laplace equation says that the sum of second coordinate derivatives vanishes. It models equilibrium potentials and constrains local behavior.

6.1.2 · Mean-value property

A harmonic function equals its average on sufficiently small circles or disks. This yields maximum principles and uniqueness tools.

6.1.3 · Harmonic conjugates

A harmonic conjugate pairs with a harmonic function to form a holomorphic function. Global existence can fail when the domain has nontrivial topology.

6.2 · Poisson representation

Poisson representation constructs harmonic functions from boundary data. The disk provides an explicit setting for solving a boundary-value problem.

6.2.1 · Poisson kernel

The Poisson kernel weights boundary data according to an interior point. Its positivity and normalization explain harmonic averaging.

6.2.2 · Dirichlet problem on disks

For continuous boundary data on a disk, the Poisson integral gives the harmonic extension. The maximum principle establishes uniqueness.

6.2.3 · Boundary limits

Boundary-limit theorems recover prescribed data in appropriate senses. The precise conclusion depends on the regularity of that data.

7 · Normal families

Locally uniform convergence preserves key holomorphic structure. Normal families have locally uniformly convergent subsequences in the appropriate function class.

7.1 · Compact convergence

Locally uniform convergence preserves key holomorphic structure. It is the natural convergence mode for analytic compactness arguments.

7.1.1 · Locally uniform convergence

Locally uniform convergence is uniform convergence on every compact subset. It controls functions without requiring one global bound on an open domain.

7.1.2 · Weierstrass theorem

Weierstrass' convergence theorem preserves holomorphicity under locally uniform limits. Derivatives also converge locally uniformly in this setting.

7.1.3 · Hurwitz theorem

Hurwitz's theorem constrains zeros of limits of holomorphic functions. It helps show that injectivity or nonvanishing survives suitable limiting arguments.

7.2 · Montel theory

Normal families have locally uniformly convergent subsequences in the appropriate function class. Montel's theorem supplies a central compactness criterion.

7.2.1 · Equicontinuity and compactness

Equicontinuity and local bounds enable compactness on compact subsets. A diagonal selection then handles an exhaustion of the domain.

7.2.2 · Normality criterion

A locally bounded family of holomorphic functions is normal. Cauchy estimates connect the boundedness assumption to equicontinuity.

7.2.3 · Applications

Normal-family arguments construct extremal maps and prove convergence of analytic approximations. Each application must identify the limit class and normalization.

8 · Riemann mapping theorem

An extremal proof of Riemann mapping optimizes a normalized conformal candidate. The final proof step shows that the extremal map covers the disk.

8.1 · Extremal construction

An extremal proof of Riemann mapping optimizes a normalized conformal candidate. Compactness produces an optimizer on a simply connected proper plane domain.

8.1.1 · Normalized family

The candidate family consists of injective holomorphic maps with fixed basepoint normalization. These conditions remove irrelevant automorphism freedom.

8.1.2 · Compactness

Normality supplies a convergent subsequence of candidate maps. Additional arguments ensure that its limit remains nonconstant and injective.

8.1.3 · Derivative maximization

Maximizing the derivative at the basepoint selects an extremal map. The extremal property later rules out omitted points of the disk.

8.2 · Completion and uniqueness

The final proof step shows that the extremal map covers the disk. Disk automorphisms then yield uniqueness under the chosen normalization.

8.2.1 · Surjectivity argument

A map omitting an interior disk point can be modified to improve the extremal derivative. The resulting contradiction proves surjectivity.

8.2.2 · Omitted-value method

The omitted-value construction uses automorphisms and a holomorphic root on a simply connected domain. This is where simple connectivity enters decisively.

8.2.3 · Normalization uniqueness

Two normalized conformal maps differ by a normalized disk automorphism. Schwarz's lemma forces that automorphism to be the identity.

Part III · Further Theory and Applications

9 · Entire and meromorphic functions

Growth restrictions strongly limit entire functions. Infinite products and meromorphic maps extend polynomial factorization ideas.

9.1 · Growth and factorization

Growth restrictions strongly limit entire functions. Integral identities connect the size of a function to its zeros.

9.1.1 · Liouville theorem

Liouville's theorem states that a bounded entire function is constant. Cauchy derivative estimates give a short proof.

9.1.2 · Maximum modulus

The maximum-modulus principle excludes a strict interior maximum for a nonconstant holomorphic function. It controls extremal behavior on bounded domains.

9.1.3 · Jensen formula

Jensen's formula relates average logarithmic boundary size to interior zeros. It introduces a quantitative connection between growth and zero distribution.

9.2 · Products and value distribution

Infinite products and meromorphic maps extend polynomial factorization ideas. Selected value-distribution results illustrate the reach of complex rigidity.

9.2.1 · Weierstrass products

Weierstrass products construct entire functions with prescribed discrete zeros and multiplicities. Convergence factors compensate for zeros escaping to infinity.

9.2.2 · Meromorphic maps

Meromorphic functions are holomorphic maps to the Riemann sphere. This viewpoint treats poles as ordinary preimages of infinity.

9.2.3 · Selected Picard themes

Picard's theorems place strong restrictions on omitted values of entire functions and essential singularities. Their precise statements distinguish local singular behavior from global entire behavior.

10 · Riemann surfaces and advanced topics

Analytic continuation extends local analytic data along paths. The final section introduces several directions beyond one-variable core theory.

10.1 · Analytic continuation

Analytic continuation extends local analytic data along paths. Riemann surfaces record the geometry created when continuation becomes multivalued.

10.1.1 · Monodromy

Monodromy studies how continuation changes around loops. A simply connected domain removes this ambiguity under the monodromy theorem's hypotheses.

10.1.2 · Branched coverings

Branched coverings model functions with locally multiple sheets. Branch points explain failures of ordinary covering behavior.

10.1.3 · Local coordinates

Holomorphic coordinate charts define a Riemann surface. Compatible charts allow analytic concepts to be transported across the surface.

10.2 · Further directions

The final section introduces several directions beyond one-variable core theory. Each route requires additional analytic or geometric tools.

10.2.1 · Elliptic functions

Elliptic functions are doubly periodic meromorphic functions on the plane. They connect complex tori to algebraic curves.

10.2.2 · Hardy spaces

Hardy spaces control holomorphic functions through boundary-integrability norms. They provide a setting for harmonic and operator-theoretic applications.

10.2.3 · Several complex variables or potential theory

Several complex variables and potential theory extend the one-variable toolkit in different directions. Their new phenomena show which planar arguments do not generalize directly.
