Part I · Foundations and Core Language

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

1 · Affine varieties and coordinate rings

Affine algebraic geometry studies polynomial zero sets. Coordinate rings encode polynomial functions on algebraic sets.

1.1 · Algebraic sets

Affine algebraic geometry studies polynomial zero sets. Its topology and maps are chosen to retain algebraic information.

1.1.1 · Zariski topology

Zariski closed sets are common zero sets of polynomial families. The resulting topology is typically much coarser than Euclidean topology.

1.1.2 · Irreducibility

An irreducible space cannot be written as the union of two proper closed subsets. Irreducible components are the basic geometric pieces of a variety.

1.1.3 · Polynomial maps

Polynomial maps are maps whose coordinate functions are polynomials on the source. They provide the initial notion of algebraic morphism.

1.2 · Coordinate algebras

Coordinate rings encode polynomial functions on algebraic sets. Their ideals make geometric containment and decomposition accessible algebraically.

1.2.1 · Ideals and quotients

Quotienting a polynomial ring by the vanishing ideal identifies functions equal on the set. The quotient is its coordinate ring.

1.2.2 · Radical ideals

A radical ideal contains every element whose positive power it contains. Over an algebraically closed field, radical ideals correspond to affine algebraic sets.

1.2.3 · Algebraic set–ideal correspondence

Taking zero sets and vanishing ideals reverses inclusions. The Nullstellensatz will state exactly when these operations recover the original data.

2 · Nullstellensatz and morphisms

Noetherianity makes algebraic geometry finite despite infinite polynomial rings of equations. The Nullstellensatz connects points and ideals over an algebraically closed field.

2.1 · Hilbert basis theorem

Noetherianity makes algebraic geometry finite despite infinite polynomial rings of equations. Hilbert's basis theorem is the main finiteness input.

2.1.1 · Noetherianity

A Noetherian ring has every ideal finitely generated. This condition controls the complexity of equations and algebraic decompositions.

2.1.2 · Ascending chains

The ascending-chain condition says that increasing chains of ideals eventually stabilize. It is equivalent to Noetherianity.

2.1.3 · Finite generation

Finite generation replaces potentially infinite equation systems by finite generating sets. This is the algebraic basis for many finite constructions.

2.2 · Nullstellensatz

The Nullstellensatz connects points and ideals over an algebraically closed field. Its weak and strong forms support the algebra-geometry dictionary.

2.2.1 · Weak and strong forms

The weak form describes maximal ideals, while the strong form identifies vanishing ideals with radicals. Both depend on the base-field hypotheses.

2.2.2 · Maximal ideals

Maximal ideals of a polynomial ring over an algebraically closed field correspond to points. Over other fields, residue-field extensions become relevant.

2.2.3 · Geometric interpretation

Polynomial consequences on a zero set are encoded by membership in a radical ideal. This interprets algebraic ideal operations geometrically.

3 · Projective geometry

Projective space identifies proportional nonzero coordinate vectors. Projective varieties are zero sets of homogeneous equations.

3.1 · Projective spaces

Projective space identifies proportional nonzero coordinate vectors. It provides a setting in which affine directions at infinity become points.

3.1.1 · Homogeneous coordinates

Homogeneous coordinates are defined only up to a common nonzero scalar. Formulas must respect that equivalence.

3.1.2 · Standard affine charts

Standard affine charts normalize one nonzero coordinate to one. They relate projective geometry to the affine theory already developed.

3.1.3 · Projective closure

Projective closure adds limiting directions to an affine variety through homogeneous equations. It provides a natural completion for many geometric problems.

3.2 · Projective varieties

Projective varieties are zero sets of homogeneous equations. Their global behavior motivates graded rings and proper morphisms.

3.2.1 · Homogeneous ideals

Homogeneous ideals are generated by homogeneous polynomials. They encode equations that are well defined in projective coordinates.

3.2.2 · Proj motivation

The Proj construction builds geometric objects from graded rings. It generalizes the passage from homogeneous equations to projective varieties.

3.2.3 · Properness intuition

Properness is an algebraic analogue of compactness with a precise valuative formulation. Projective morphisms supply important examples.

Part II · Central Theory and Techniques

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

4 · Dimension and local algebra

Dimension can be described by prime-ideal chains or by function-field transcendence. Local rings retain functions near a chosen point.

4.1 · Krull dimension

Dimension can be described by prime-ideal chains or by function-field transcendence. Comparing these viewpoints connects local algebra with geometry.

4.1.1 · Prime chains

Chains of prime ideals measure nested irreducible closed subsets in reverse order. Their maximal possible length defines Krull dimension.

4.1.2 · Dimension of polynomial rings

A polynomial ring in finitely many variables over a field has dimension equal to the number of variables. This calibrates dimension against affine space.

4.1.3 · Transcendence degree

Transcendence degree counts algebraically independent elements in a field extension. For irreducible affine varieties, it agrees with geometric dimension.

4.2 · Local rings

Local rings retain functions near a chosen point. Their maximal ideals and quotients measure the point's local geometry.

4.2.1 · Localization

Localization makes functions nonvanishing at a point invertible. It isolates the algebra relevant in that neighborhood.

4.2.2 · Regular local rings

A Noetherian local ring is regular when its dimension equals the minimal number of generators of its maximal ideal. This is a central algebraic test for nonsingularity.

4.2.3 · Tangent spaces

The Zariski tangent space is dual to the maximal ideal modulo its square over the residue field. Excess tangent dimension can reveal singularities.

5 · Maps, fibers, and birational geometry

Morphisms relate varieties while preserving regular functions. Birational geometry identifies varieties that agree on dense open subsets.

5.1 · Morphisms

Morphisms relate varieties while preserving regular functions. Their image and fiber properties reveal how dimensions and equations interact.

5.1.1 · Rational maps

A rational map is defined by compatible regular maps on a dense open subset. Indeterminacy distinguishes it from a globally defined morphism.

5.1.2 · Dominant and finite maps

Dominant maps have dense images, while finite maps correspond locally to finite module extensions. These properties lead to different control of fibers and function fields.

5.1.3 · Fibers

A fiber consists of points mapping to a specified target point, with an algebraic structure. Fiber geometry tracks variation across a family.

5.2 · Birational transformations

Birational geometry identifies varieties that agree on dense open subsets. It studies transformations that alter special loci while preserving function fields.

5.2.1 · Function fields

The function field records rational functions on an irreducible variety. Isomorphic function fields correspond to birational equivalence in the usual setting.

5.2.2 · Blowups

A blowup replaces a chosen center with geometric direction data. It can separate intersections or simplify some singularities.

5.2.3 · Resolution motivation

Resolution seeks a smooth model related to a singular variety by an appropriate birational map. Existence and scope depend on dimension and characteristic.

6 · Algebraic curves

Algebraic curves provide a tractable laboratory for local and global geometry. Divisors record zeros and poles with integer multiplicities.

6.1 · Smooth and singular curves

Algebraic curves provide a tractable laboratory for local and global geometry. Smoothness and normalization explain how singular models relate to simpler ones.

6.1.1 · Local parameters

At a smooth point of a curve, a local parameter measures vanishing to first order. It makes the local ring resemble a discrete valuation ring.

6.1.2 · Normalization

Normalization replaces a curve by a normal model with the same function field. It separates certain branches hidden by singularities.

6.1.3 · Singularity examples

Nodes and cusps display different local singular behavior. Comparing their local rings and tangent cones makes those differences precise.

6.2 · Divisors on curves

Divisors record zeros and poles with integer multiplicities. They connect rational functions to global geometric invariants of curves.

6.2.1 · Orders of vanishing

Order of vanishing measures the power of a local parameter in a function. Negative order records a pole.

6.2.2 · Principal divisors

A principal divisor lists the zeros and poles of a nonzero rational function. Such divisors define the equivalence relation used in divisor class groups.

6.2.3 · Degree

The degree of a divisor sums coefficients weighted by residue-field degrees. On smooth projective curves, principal divisors have degree zero.

7 · Line bundles and Riemann–Roch

Line bundles are locally free sheaves of rank one. Riemann–Roch relates dimensions of section spaces to degree and genus.

7.1 · Invertible sheaves

Line bundles are locally free sheaves of rank one. They recast divisor questions as questions about sections and gluing.

7.1.1 · Divisors and line bundles

A divisor on a smooth curve determines a line bundle by allowing prescribed poles. This construction links divisor classes with isomorphism classes of line bundles.

7.1.2 · Sections

A global section is a compatible choice of local sections. Its existence and zeros encode global constraints on a line bundle.

7.1.3 · Degree

The degree of a line bundle on a smooth projective curve is defined through an associated divisor. It influences the number of independent sections.

7.2 · Riemann–Roch theorem

Riemann–Roch relates dimensions of section spaces to degree and genus. It is a central bridge from local divisor data to global geometry.

7.2.1 · Statement and examples

The curve theorem compares sections of a divisor and its complementary canonical divisor. Examples show how degree and genus constrain rational functions.

7.2.2 · Canonical divisor

The canonical divisor corresponds to meromorphic differentials on a smooth curve. It has degree twice the genus minus two.

7.2.3 · Applications to embeddings

Sufficiently positive divisors produce maps and often embeddings into projective space. Riemann–Roch provides the section counts needed to establish these properties.

8 · Sheaves and cohomology

Sheaves organize local data together with compatible restrictions. Sheaf cohomology measures failures of global lifting and gluing.

8.1 · Sheaf language

Sheaves organize local data together with compatible restrictions. They allow definitions and constructions to be glued across open covers.

8.1.1 · Presheaves

A presheaf assigns data to open sets with compatible restriction maps. It does not yet require local data to glue uniquely.

8.1.2 · Sheafification

Sheafification turns a presheaf into a sheaf with the corresponding local information. Its universal property controls maps into other sheaves.

8.1.3 · Structure sheaf

The structure sheaf records regular functions on each open set. Together with the underlying space, it carries the essential algebraic geometry.

8.2 · Cohomology

Sheaf cohomology measures failures of global lifting and gluing. Computations and finiteness results turn those obstructions into usable invariants.

8.2.1 · Čech computations

Čech cohomology uses intersections of an open cover to build a cochain complex. Suitable covers allow explicit calculations of sheaf cohomology.

8.2.2 · Exact sequences

Short exact sequences of sheaves induce long exact cohomology sequences. These connect local algebraic relations to global obstruction groups.

8.2.3 · Vanishing and finiteness themes

Finiteness and vanishing theorems require conditions such as properness, coherence, or positivity. Their conclusions make cohomology computable in important cases.

Part III · Further Theory and Applications

9 · Schemes and scheme morphisms

The spectrum construction extends varieties to general commutative rings. Schemes are assembled by gluing affine pieces with compatible sheaves.

9.1 · Spectrum of a ring

The spectrum construction extends varieties to general commutative rings. A scheme retains both prime-ideal topology and local rings.

9.1.1 · Prime spectrum

The prime spectrum consists of prime ideals with the Zariski topology. Its points include generic points and arithmetic primes.

9.1.2 · Structure sheaf

The spectrum's structure sheaf is built from localizations on basic open sets. Its stalk at a prime is the corresponding local ring.

9.1.3 · Affine schemes

An affine scheme is a prime spectrum with its structure sheaf. General schemes are locally of this form.

9.2 · Gluing and maps

Schemes are assembled by gluing affine pieces with compatible sheaves. Morphisms and fiber products describe maps and families in this broader category.

9.2.1 · Scheme morphisms

A scheme morphism is a suitable map of locally ringed spaces. The condition on stalks ensures compatibility with local algebra.

9.2.2 · Fiber products

Fiber products represent pairs of points or morphisms agreeing over a base. They give the correct construction of fibers and intersections over that base.

9.2.3 · Base change

Base change replaces the parameter scheme while pulling back a family. It is essential for comparing geometry over different fields or rings.

10 · Smoothness, intersection, and advanced directions

Smoothness and singularity criteria connect equations with local algebra. Intersection and moduli theory extend the course toward global classification problems.

10.1 · Smoothness and singularities

Smoothness and singularity criteria connect equations with local algebra. Their precise equivalences depend on the base field and finiteness assumptions.

10.1.1 · Jacobian criterion

The Jacobian criterion tests smoothness in suitable finite-type settings using ranks of derivatives. Over imperfect fields, regularity and smoothness require careful distinction.

10.1.2 · Tangent cone

The tangent cone records leading-order local equations at a singular point. It contains more information than the tangent space alone.

10.1.3 · Regularity

Regularity means regular local rings at the points under consideration. Over a perfect field in the standard finite-type setting, it aligns with smoothness.

10.2 · Intersection and moduli

Intersection and moduli theory extend the course toward global classification problems. Arithmetic and étale methods introduce additional structures beyond classical varieties.

10.2.1 · Intersection numbers

Intersection numbers quantify how geometric cycles meet with multiplicity. Properness and intersection hypotheses determine which computations are valid.

10.2.2 · Moduli motivation

A moduli problem seeks a geometric parameter space for objects up to isomorphism. Automorphisms and families make this subtler than listing individual objects.

10.2.3 · Étale or arithmetic geometry topics

Étale geometry studies a refined notion of local structure, while arithmetic geometry studies schemes over arithmetic bases. Both expand the tools available for global questions.
