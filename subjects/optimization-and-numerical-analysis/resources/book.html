Part I · Foundations and Core Language

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

1 · Mathematical modeling and numerical reliability

Optimization begins by specifying decisions, admissible choices, and a scalar objective. Finite-precision representation introduces errors before an algorithm finishes its first step.

1.1 · Optimization models

Optimization begins by specifying decisions, admissible choices, and a scalar objective. This mathematical model determines which existence and algorithmic results can apply.

1.1.1 · Variables and constraints

Variables represent the choices to be made, while constraints express admissibility. Their domains and units should be fixed before analysis.

1.1.2 · Feasible sets

The feasible set contains all points satisfying the constraints. Its geometry determines whether minimizers can exist or be reached.

1.1.3 · Objective geometry

Objective geometry includes convexity, smoothness, and level-set structure. These properties influence uniqueness, optimality conditions, and algorithms.

1.2 · Numerical representation

Finite-precision representation introduces errors before an algorithm finishes its first step. Reliable computation distinguishes these errors from mathematical approximation.

1.2.1 · Floating-point arithmetic

Floating-point numbers represent a finite set of scaled mantissas and exponents. Their spacing depends on magnitude.

1.2.2 · Rounding

Rounding replaces an exact value by a nearby representable number. Repeated operations can accumulate or amplify those errors.

1.2.3 · Stable and unstable formulations

Equivalent formulas can have very different sensitivity to rounding and cancellation. Stable formulations avoid unnecessary error amplification.

2 · Linear and convex optimization

Linear programming optimizes a linear function over linear constraints. Duality associates a bounding problem with an original optimization problem.

2.1 · Linear programming

Linear programming optimizes a linear function over linear constraints. Polyhedral geometry connects algorithms with certificates of optimality.

2.1.1 · Polyhedra

A polyhedron is an intersection of finitely many linear half-spaces and hyperplanes. Its faces and extreme points organize linear objectives.

2.1.2 · Simplex method

The simplex method moves among basic feasible solutions while improving an objective. Degeneracy and pivot rules affect its behavior.

2.1.3 · Optimality certificates

Primal and dual feasible points with equal objective values certify optimality. A certificate can be checked independently of how the points were found.

2.2 · Duality

Duality associates a bounding problem with an original optimization problem. Equality of optimal values gives strong certificates under appropriate hypotheses.

2.2.1 · Weak and strong duality

Weak duality gives an objective bound for every feasible primal-dual pair. Strong duality identifies conditions under which the bound is attained or exact.

2.2.2 · Complementary slackness

Complementary slackness pairs inactive constraints with zero multipliers. Together with feasibility, it characterizes optimal pairs in standard linear programming.

2.2.3 · Sensitivity

Dual variables quantify changes in the optimal value under suitable perturbations. Sensitivity interpretations require attention to differentiability and stability.

3 · Convex analysis

Convexity makes local geometric information powerful enough for global conclusions. Subgradients extend derivative-based reasoning to nonsmooth convex functions.

3.1 · Convex sets and functions

Convexity makes local geometric information powerful enough for global conclusions. Separation theorems describe convex sets through linear inequalities.

3.1.1 · Separation

Separation places disjoint convex sets on different sides of an affine functional under suitable assumptions. It is a foundation for duality and optimality theory.

3.1.2 · Epigraphs

The epigraph contains points above a function's graph. A function is convex exactly when its epigraph is convex.

3.1.3 · Supporting hyperplanes

A supporting hyperplane touches a convex set without cutting through its interior. It gives linear information at boundary points.

3.2 · Subgradients and conjugates

Subgradients extend derivative-based reasoning to nonsmooth convex functions. Conjugacy and proximal maps turn this geometry into algorithms.

3.2.1 · Fenchel conjugacy

The Fenchel conjugate represents a function through affine lower bounds. It produces dual formulations and inequality-based characterizations.

3.2.2 · Subdifferentials

A subgradient defines a supporting affine function to a convex objective. The collection of all such vectors is the subdifferential.

3.2.3 · Proximal maps

A proximal map minimizes a function plus a quadratic distance penalty. It supplies an implicit step suited to nonsmooth regularizers.

Part II · Central Theory and Techniques

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

4 · Constrained nonlinear optimization

Equality-constrained optimality compares objective derivatives with the geometry of a level set. KKT conditions add inequality feasibility, nonnegative multipliers, and complementary slackness to stationarity.

4.1 · Lagrange multipliers

Equality-constrained optimality compares objective derivatives with the geometry of a level set. Regularity is needed to derive multiplier equations.

4.1.1 · Equality constraints

Equality constraints define the feasible set through equations. Their derivative kernels describe feasible first-order directions at regular points.

4.1.2 · Regularity

A standard regularity condition requires independent constraint gradients. It makes the feasible set locally smooth and justifies multiplier necessity.

4.1.3 · Second-order conditions

Second-order conditions examine curvature along feasible tangent directions. The appropriate Hessian is that of the Lagrangian rather than the objective alone.

4.2 · KKT theory

KKT conditions add inequality feasibility, nonnegative multipliers, and complementary slackness to stationarity. Their necessity and sufficiency require different hypotheses.

4.2.1 · Inequality constraints

Inequality constraints restrict which local directions remain feasible. Active constraints determine the boundary geometry relevant to stationarity.

4.2.2 · Constraint qualifications

Constraint qualifications exclude degeneracies that can prevent valid multipliers from existing. Different qualifications cover different nonlinear settings.

4.2.3 · Saddle-point interpretation

A suitable primal-dual saddle point expresses both minimization and bounding behavior. In convex settings it connects KKT conditions with dual optimality.

5 · First-order optimization algorithms

Gradient methods use first derivatives to generate improving iterates. Composite optimization separates smooth and nonsmooth terms.

5.1 · Gradient methods

Gradient methods use first derivatives to generate improving iterates. Their guarantees depend on smoothness, step choice, and objective structure.

5.1.1 · Descent lemma

The descent lemma bounds objective change using a Lipschitz gradient. It supplies a sufficient decrease estimate for gradient steps.

5.1.2 · Step-size rules

Fixed, diminishing, and line-search step rules balance progress with stability. Their admissibility depends on the convergence theorem used.

5.1.3 · Convergence rates

Convergence rates quantify how error decreases with iteration count. Convexity and strong convexity lead to different standard rates.

5.2 · Proximal and accelerated methods

Composite optimization separates smooth and nonsmooth terms. Proximal steps and momentum use that structure to improve convergence behavior.

5.2.1 · Composite objectives

A composite objective is a sum whose terms admit different useful operations. Typical examples combine a smooth loss with a nonsmooth regularizer.

5.2.2 · Proximal gradient

Proximal gradient combines a gradient step for the smooth term with a proximal step for the other term. Its analysis balances smoothness with convexity or specified nonconvex assumptions.

5.2.3 · Acceleration principles

Acceleration introduces carefully designed extrapolation or momentum. Faster rates require matching the algorithm to the assumptions of the guarantee.

6 · Second-order and large-scale methods

Newton methods use local curvature to solve stationarity equations. Trust-region methods minimize a local model only where it is considered reliable.

6.1 · Newton methods

Newton methods use local curvature to solve stationarity equations. Their rapid local behavior needs modification far from a solution.

6.1.1 · Local convergence

Under suitable smoothness and nonsingularity, Newton iteration converges rapidly near a solution. The initial neighborhood is part of the local claim.

6.1.2 · Damping

Damping scales the Newton step to secure improvement or maintain feasibility. It connects local curvature information with global safeguards.

6.1.3 · Quasi-Newton updates

Quasi-Newton updates approximate curvature using changes in gradients and iterates. Their update conditions aim to retain useful symmetry and positivity.

6.2 · Trust-region methods

Trust-region methods minimize a local model only where it is considered reliable. Comparing predicted and actual improvement controls the region size.

6.2.1 · Models and subproblems

A trust-region subproblem constrains a local model step to a prescribed neighborhood. Its solution balances model accuracy with objective improvement.

6.2.2 · Acceptance ratios

The acceptance ratio compares actual reduction with predicted reduction. It determines whether to accept the step and how to change the radius.

6.2.3 · Globalization

Globalization seeks convergence from a broader set of initial points than local Newton theory allows. It generally means convergence to stationary points under stated assumptions, not automatic global minimization.

7 · Approximation and interpolation

Approximation replaces a function by a simpler polynomial model. Piecewise polynomials achieve flexibility through local constructions.

7.1 · Polynomial approximation

Approximation replaces a function by a simpler polynomial model. Error estimates explain how nodes, degree, and smoothness affect accuracy.

7.1.1 · Interpolation error

Interpolation error measures the difference between a function and the polynomial matching its data. Bounds depend on smoothness and node placement.

7.1.2 · Chebyshev ideas

Chebyshev nodes and polynomial bases control oscillation and approximation behavior. They mitigate difficulties associated with poorly chosen equispaced nodes.

7.1.3 · Least-squares approximation

Least-squares approximation minimizes an aggregate squared discrepancy over a chosen approximation space. It differs from requiring exact interpolation at every data point.

7.2 · Splines and approximation spaces

Piecewise polynomials achieve flexibility through local constructions. Continuity conditions and approximation order determine their usefulness.

7.2.1 · Piecewise polynomials

Piecewise polynomials assign separate polynomial expressions to subintervals or cells. Local degree and interface matching control regularity.

7.2.2 · Splines

Splines join polynomial pieces with prescribed smoothness. Their local bases support stable approximation and interpolation.

7.2.3 · Approximation order

Approximation order describes how error scales with mesh size or polynomial degree. It depends on both the approximation space and target regularity.

8 · Numerical linear algebra

Direct linear solvers factor a matrix and solve simpler systems. Iterative linear solvers build approximations through repeated matrix actions.

8.1 · Direct methods

Direct linear solvers factor a matrix and solve simpler systems. Stability analysis explains whether the result corresponds to nearby data.

8.1.1 · Gaussian elimination

Gaussian elimination removes variables through row operations. Pivoting is often needed to control numerical growth.

8.1.2 · LU, QR, and Cholesky

LU, QR, and Cholesky exploit different matrix structures. Their assumptions and error properties determine which factorization is appropriate.

8.1.3 · Stability

Numerical stability concerns the effect of rounding within the algorithm. Backward-error analysis distinguishes it from sensitivity intrinsic to the matrix problem.

8.2 · Iterative methods

Iterative linear solvers build approximations through repeated matrix actions. Krylov spaces and preconditioning can make large problems tractable.

8.2.1 · Krylov subspaces

A Krylov subspace is generated by successive matrix powers acting on an initial residual. It restricts an approximation problem to increasingly rich subspaces.

8.2.2 · Conjugate gradients

Conjugate gradients solves symmetric positive definite systems by minimizing error over Krylov subspaces. Spectral conditioning strongly affects its convergence.

8.2.3 · Preconditioning

A preconditioner transforms the system to improve iterative behavior. Its benefit must be balanced against the cost of applying it.

Part III · Further Theory and Applications

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

9 · Quadrature, differentiation, and ODEs

Quadrature approximates an integral by weighted function values. Numerical ODE methods approximate continuous evolution by discrete steps.

9.1 · Numerical integration

Quadrature approximates an integral by weighted function values. Polynomial exactness and error estimation guide the choice of nodes and weights.

9.1.1 · Newton–Cotes

Newton–Cotes formulas use polynomial interpolation at typically equally spaced nodes. High-order formulas can suffer from unfavorable stability and error behavior.

9.1.2 · Gaussian quadrature

Gaussian quadrature chooses nodes and weights to maximize polynomial exactness for a given measure. Orthogonal polynomials determine the nodes.

9.1.3 · Adaptive error control

Adaptive quadrature subdivides or adjusts effort using local error indicators. Reliable stopping requires indicators that reflect the actual integrand behavior.

9.2 · ODE methods

Numerical ODE methods approximate continuous evolution by discrete steps. Stability and stiffness determine which step sizes are practical.

9.2.1 · Runge–Kutta

Runge–Kutta methods combine several derivative evaluations per step. Their order conditions control local and global truncation error.

9.2.2 · Stability regions

A method's stability region describes allowable scaled test-equation eigenvalues. It helps explain when numerical solutions damp or amplify modes.

9.2.3 · Stiffness and adaptive stepping

Stiff problems contain time scales that severely restrict explicit steps. Implicit methods and adaptive control address the resulting stability and accuracy tradeoffs.

10 · Numerical PDE and computational practice

Numerical PDE methods combine discretization with analytic estimates. Computational practice should produce results that can be independently checked.

10.1 · Finite differences and elements

Numerical PDE methods combine discretization with analytic estimates. Convergence arguments must separate approximation accuracy from numerical stability.

10.1.1 · Consistency

Consistency measures how well a discrete equation approximates the continuous equation on sufficiently regular functions. It alone does not ensure convergent solutions.

10.1.2 · Stability

Stability controls how discrete solutions respond to data and perturbations. Its norm and uniformity requirements must match the convergence analysis.

10.1.3 · Convergence

Convergence means that numerical solutions approach the specified continuous solution under refinement. Standard equivalence theorems apply only within their stated problem classes.

10.1.4 · Variational formulation

A variational formulation expresses a PDE through integrals and test spaces. Finite elements discretize those spaces while preserving useful structure.

10.2 · Reproducible computation

Computational practice should produce results that can be independently checked. Benchmarks, uncertainty estimates, and stopping rules supply that evidence.

10.2.1 · Verification and validation

Verification checks whether the numerical method solves its stated equations correctly, while validation concerns suitability for the intended model or application. These are distinct evidence questions.

10.2.2 · Benchmarks

Benchmarks compare an implementation against analytic solutions or independently trusted references. They should exercise the actual numerical difficulties of interest.

10.2.3 · Uncertainty and stopping criteria

Stopping criteria translate residuals or changes into justified error information when possible. Uncertainty reporting should distinguish discretization, rounding, iteration, and model effects.
