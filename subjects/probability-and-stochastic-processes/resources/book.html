Part I · Foundations and Core Language

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

1 · Probability spaces and random variables

Probability uses measure theory to assign consistent weights to events. Expectation integrates random quantities, while independence controls how joint events factor.

1.1 · Measure-theoretic foundations

Probability uses measure theory to assign consistent weights to events. Random variables transport that structure into observable quantities.

1.1.1 · Probability spaces

A probability space has a sigma-algebra of events and a measure of total mass one. It is the base setting for all probabilistic statements.

1.1.2 · Measurable random variables

A random variable is a measurable map into a specified state space. Measurability makes questions about its values into measurable events.

1.1.3 · Distributions

The distribution is the pushforward probability measure of a random variable. It records probabilities of observable values independently of the original sample space.

1.2 · Expectation and independence

Expectation integrates random quantities, while independence controls how joint events factor. Product spaces make this interaction concrete.

1.2.1 · Integration

Expectation is the integral of a random variable when defined. Integrability conditions determine which algebraic manipulations are justified.

1.2.2 · Product measures

Product measures construct joint laws with independent coordinates. They support the canonical models for repeated independent experiments.

1.2.3 · Independence and conditioning basics

Independence factors joint probabilities, while conditioning updates information relative to an event or sigma-algebra. The two notions interact but should not be conflated.

2 · Convergence and limit laws

Different probabilistic convergence modes retain different information about random variables. Laws of large numbers explain why empirical averages stabilize.

2.1 · Modes of convergence

Different probabilistic convergence modes retain different information about random variables. Tracking their implications is essential in limit theorems.

2.1.1 · Almost sure

Almost-sure convergence means pointwise convergence on an event of probability one. It describes the limiting behavior of almost every sample path.

2.1.2 · In probability

Convergence in probability makes the chance of a fixed-size error tend to zero. It is weaker than almost-sure convergence in general.

2.1.3 · In Lp

Lp convergence makes the expected pth power of the error tend to zero for finite p. It controls moments more strongly than convergence in probability.

2.1.4 · In distribution

Convergence in distribution means weak convergence of the probability laws. It can hold even when variables are defined on different sample spaces.

2.2 · Laws of large numbers

Laws of large numbers explain why empirical averages stabilize. Their mode of convergence and moment assumptions determine the exact conclusion.

2.2.1 · Borel–Cantelli

Borel–Cantelli relates summability of event probabilities to repeated occurrence. Its converse form needs additional assumptions such as independence.

2.2.2 · Weak law

A weak law gives convergence of averages in probability under specified conditions. It justifies large-sample concentration around a mean.

2.2.3 · Strong law

A strong law gives almost-sure convergence of averages under suitable hypotheses. It makes a stronger sample-path statement than a weak law.

3 · Characteristic functions and central limits

Characteristic functions encode probability distributions by complex exponentials. Central limit theorems describe normalized fluctuations around average behavior.

3.1 · Characteristic functions

Characteristic functions encode probability distributions by complex exponentials. Fourier-analytic properties make them effective tools for weak convergence.

3.1.1 · Fourier transforms

The characteristic function is the Fourier transform of a probability measure with a fixed sign convention. Independence turns sums into products of transforms.

3.1.2 · Uniqueness

A probability law is uniquely determined by its characteristic function. This allows distributional identities to be proved in transform space.

3.1.3 · Continuity theorem

Lévy's continuity theorem connects weak convergence with characteristic-function convergence under the continuity-at-zero condition. It is a main bridge from analytic limits to probability limits.

3.2 · Central limit theory

Central limit theorems describe normalized fluctuations around average behavior. Their generalizations clarify which dependence and tail conditions are admissible.

3.2.1 · Classical CLT

The classical independent identically distributed CLT assumes a finite nonzero variance. Centered normalized sums then converge in distribution to a Gaussian law.

3.2.2 · Triangular arrays

Triangular arrays allow the summands to change with the sample size. Lindeberg- or Lyapunov-type conditions control the resulting fluctuation limit.

3.2.3 · Selected refinements

Refinements can quantify convergence rates or weaken standard assumptions. Each refinement must state which moments, independence, or dependence conditions it requires.

Part II · Central Theory and Techniques

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

4 · Conditional expectation and martingales

Conditional expectation is an integrable quantity adapted to a chosen information sigma-algebra. Martingales formalize processes whose conditional future mean equals their present value.

4.1 · Conditional expectation

Conditional expectation is an integrable quantity adapted to a chosen information sigma-algebra. It preserves averages over all events in that information set.

4.1.1 · Radon–Nikodym definition

The Radon–Nikodym theorem constructs conditional expectation through its defining integral identities. The result is unique up to almost-sure equality.

4.1.2 · Tower property

The tower property expresses consistency when conditioning through nested sigma-algebras. It is a basic tool in iterative information arguments.

4.1.3 · Conditional distributions

Conditional distributions describe laws given observed information when appropriate regular versions exist. Their existence is not automatic on arbitrary measurable spaces.

4.2 · Martingale basics

Martingales formalize processes whose conditional future mean equals their present value. Filtrations and stopping times make the information structure explicit.

4.2.1 · Filtrations

A filtration is an increasing family of sigma-algebras. It records information available as time passes.

4.2.2 · Adaptedness

A process is adapted when its value at each time is measurable using current information. Adaptedness prevents dependence on future observations.

4.2.3 · Examples and stopping times

Random walks and conditional-expectation processes give standard martingales. Stopping times model decisions determined by the information available when stopping occurs.

5 · Martingale theorems

Martingale inequalities control maxima and oscillations without independence of every increment. Convergence and stopping theorems require careful integrability or boundedness conditions.

5.1 · Inequalities

Martingale inequalities control maxima and oscillations without independence of every increment. These bounds are the main input for convergence results.

5.1.1 · Doob inequalities

Doob inequalities bound martingale or submartingale maxima using terminal values under suitable integrability assumptions. They convert endpoint control into pathwise-probability control.

5.1.2 · Maximal estimates

Maximal estimates bound the chance or moment of large process excursions. They help establish uniform control over time.

5.1.3 · Upcrossings

Upcrossing inequalities bound how often a submartingale oscillates across a fixed interval. Finite upcrossings support almost-sure convergence arguments.

5.2 · Convergence and stopping

Convergence and stopping theorems require careful integrability or boundedness conditions. Without them, intuitively fair-process arguments can fail.

5.2.1 · Martingale convergence

Martingale convergence theorems give almost-sure or norm limits under different hypotheses. Uniform integrability is central when preserving expectations in a limit.

5.2.2 · Optional stopping hypotheses

Optional stopping requires conditions on the process and stopping time, such as suitable boundedness or integrability. An arbitrary stopping time does not preserve expectation automatically.

5.2.3 · Counterexamples

Gambling and random-walk stopping examples expose failures when hypotheses are omitted. Each counterexample identifies the step where an unjustified limit enters.

6 · Markov chains and processes

A discrete-time Markov chain evolves through transition probabilities depending only on the current state. Continuous-time Markov models describe evolution without a fixed time step.

6.1 · Discrete-time chains

A discrete-time Markov chain evolves through transition probabilities depending only on the current state. Long-run behavior is organized by communication and recurrence.

6.1.1 · Transition matrices

A transition matrix records one-step probabilities between countable states. Matrix powers describe multiple steps.

6.1.2 · Recurrence

Recurrence means return to a state occurs with probability one from that state. Positive recurrence additionally controls the expected return time.

6.1.3 · Invariant measures

An invariant measure is unchanged by the transition operator. When normalizable to a probability, it is a stationary distribution.

6.2 · Continuous-time processes

Continuous-time Markov models describe evolution without a fixed time step. Generators and semigroups relate infinitesimal rates to finite-time transition laws.

6.2.1 · Generators

A generator describes the initial rate of change of the evolution operator. Its domain is an essential part of the definition.

6.2.2 · Jump chains

A jump chain records successive states visited by a jump process. Holding times supply the additional timing information.

6.2.3 · Semigroups

A Markov semigroup composes transition operators according to elapsed time. It encodes the same evolution at every time scale.

7 · Poisson and renewal processes

The Poisson process models event arrivals at a constant rate. Renewal theory generalizes Poisson arrivals by allowing nonexponential waiting times.

7.1 · Poisson process

The Poisson process models event arrivals at a constant rate. Its independent stationary increments make it a fundamental continuous-time example.

7.1.1 · Construction

A Poisson process can be constructed from independent exponential waiting times. This connects counting probabilities to interarrival-time distributions.

7.1.2 · Independent increments

Counts on disjoint time intervals are independent in a Poisson process. Their means depend only on interval lengths and the rate.

7.1.3 · Thinning and superposition

Independent thinning preserves the Poisson form, and independent superposition adds rates. These operations support decomposition and construction of arrival models.

7.2 · Renewal theory

Renewal theory generalizes Poisson arrivals by allowing nonexponential waiting times. It studies the accumulation and long-run frequency of renewal events.

7.2.1 · Renewal equation

The renewal equation relates a renewal quantity to the waiting-time distribution through convolution. It organizes repeated-event calculations recursively.

7.2.2 · Reward processes

A renewal reward process accumulates a reward in each cycle. Under suitable integrability, its long-run rate is expected reward divided by expected cycle length.

7.2.3 · Limit behavior

Renewal limit theorems describe asymptotic counts and residual waiting behavior. Arithmetic versus nonarithmetic waiting-time laws can change the formulation.

8 · Brownian motion

Brownian motion combines Gaussian increments with continuous sample paths. Brownian martingale tools connect path properties with stopping and variation.

8.1 · Construction and path properties

Brownian motion combines Gaussian increments with continuous sample paths. These properties create a canonical model of continuous random fluctuation.

8.1.1 · Gaussian increments

Brownian increments over disjoint intervals are independent and Gaussian with variance proportional to elapsed time. This determines the finite-dimensional laws.

8.1.2 · Continuity

Brownian motion has continuous versions despite its highly irregular paths. Continuity does not imply ordinary differentiability.

8.1.3 · Scaling

Brownian scaling rescales time quadratically relative to space. It explains the diffusion scale of the process.

8.2 · Martingale characterization

Brownian martingale tools connect path properties with stopping and variation. They prepare the construction of stochastic integration.

8.2.1 · Quadratic variation

Quadratic variation sums squared increments and converges to elapsed time for Brownian motion in the standard sense. It replaces the vanishing quadratic variation of smooth paths.

8.2.2 · Reflection principle

The reflection principle relates a Brownian running maximum to terminal Gaussian probabilities. It uses path symmetry after a suitable hitting time.

8.2.3 · Stopping arguments

Stopping arguments analyze paths at random information-compatible times. Optional stopping and strong Markov hypotheses must be checked in each use.

Part III · Further Theory and Applications

9 · Stochastic integration and SDEs

The Itô integral integrates adapted stochastic processes against Brownian motion. Itô calculus accounts for the nonzero quadratic variation of stochastic paths.

9.1 · Itô integral

The Itô integral integrates adapted stochastic processes against Brownian motion. It is constructed by an isometry rather than ordinary pathwise variation.

9.1.1 · Simple adapted processes

Simple adapted integrands are piecewise constant with values determined at the left endpoints. Their stochastic integrals are finite sums of Brownian increments.

9.1.2 · Isometry

The Itô isometry equates the integral's second moment with the expected time integral of the squared integrand. It gives the norm control needed for extension.

9.1.3 · Extension

Completion extends the integral from simple processes to an appropriate predictable square-integrable class. This preserves the isometry and martingale properties.

9.2 · Itô calculus

Itô calculus accounts for the nonzero quadratic variation of stochastic paths. It provides both transformation formulas and solution methods for SDEs.

9.2.1 · Itô formula

Itô's formula adds a second-derivative correction to the ordinary chain rule. The correction comes from quadratic variation.

9.2.2 · Existence and uniqueness for SDEs

Standard SDE existence and uniqueness theorems use coefficient conditions such as Lipschitz continuity and controlled growth. Different weak and strong notions of solution require separate statements.

9.2.3 · Examples

Geometric Brownian motion and Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes give explicit SDE solutions. They illustrate how drift and diffusion affect path and distribution behavior.

10 · Diffusions and advanced processes

Diffusion generators connect stochastic evolution with deterministic PDEs. Advanced process theory studies long-run averages, rare events, and interacting randomness.

10.1 · Generators and PDE

Diffusion generators connect stochastic evolution with deterministic PDEs. Martingale and boundary formulations provide complementary descriptions.

10.1.1 · Kolmogorov equations

Backward and forward Kolmogorov equations describe evolution of expectations and probability laws respectively. Deriving them requires matching generator and regularity assumptions.

10.1.2 · Martingale problem

A martingale problem characterizes a process through test functions and a proposed generator. Well-posedness can provide existence and uniqueness in law.

10.1.3 · Boundary behavior

Boundary behavior determines whether paths are killed, reflected, absorbed, or otherwise constrained. This affects both the stochastic model and its PDE boundary conditions.

10.2 · Further topics

Advanced process theory studies long-run averages, rare events, and interacting randomness. These routes require assumptions beyond those of elementary limit laws.

10.2.1 · Stationarity and ergodicity

Stationarity preserves the law under time shifts, while ergodicity rules out nontrivial invariant events. Together they support identification of long-time and ensemble averages in suitable theorems.

10.2.2 · Large deviations

Large deviations quantify exponentially small probabilities of atypical behavior. Rate functions replace variance as the main descriptor on that scale.

10.2.3 · Interacting systems or random graphs

Interacting particle systems and random graphs model collective random structure. Their limiting behavior introduces dependence and geometry beyond independent samples.
