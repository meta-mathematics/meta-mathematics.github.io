Part I · Foundations and Core Language

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

1 · Smooth manifolds

Charts describe a manifold locally by Euclidean coordinates. Smooth maps preserve the differentiable structures of manifolds.

1.1 · Charts and atlases

Charts describe a manifold locally by Euclidean coordinates. Compatibility of charts makes calculus independent of that choice.

1.1.1 · Smooth compatibility

Smooth compatibility requires smooth transition maps on chart overlaps. It ensures that differentiability is a property of the manifold rather than a particular chart.

1.1.2 · Maximal atlases

A maximal atlas contains all charts compatible with a given smooth atlas. It specifies the smooth structure completely.

1.1.3 · Standard examples

Spheres, tori, and projective spaces illustrate different chart constructions. They show why a manifold may require several coordinate patches.

1.2 · Smooth maps and submanifolds

Smooth maps preserve the differentiable structures of manifolds. Rank conditions describe their local images and level sets.

1.2.1 · Rank theorem

The constant-rank theorem gives a local normal form when derivative rank is constant nearby. It explains the geometry of immersions and submersions.

1.2.2 · Regular level sets

A regular level set is a preimage on which the derivative has full target rank. It inherits a smooth submanifold structure of the expected dimension.

1.2.3 · Embeddings

An embedding is an injective immersion that is a homeomorphism onto its image. It realizes an abstract manifold as a well-behaved submanifold.

2 · Tangent and cotangent geometry

Tangent spaces collect possible infinitesimal directions at points. Cotangent spaces are the duals of tangent spaces.

2.1 · Tangent spaces

Tangent spaces collect possible infinitesimal directions at points. Their equivalent descriptions make differentiation intrinsic.

2.1.1 · Derivations and curves

Tangent vectors can be represented by velocities of curves or derivations on smooth functions. The equivalence connects geometric motion with algebraic differentiation.

2.1.2 · Tangent maps

The tangent map is the derivative of a smooth map between tangent spaces. It transports velocities and satisfies the chain rule.

2.1.3 · Vector fields

A vector field assigns a tangent vector smoothly at every point. It supplies the infinitesimal data for differential equations and flows.

2.2 · Cotangent spaces

Cotangent spaces are the duals of tangent spaces. Their natural transformation rule makes differential forms and measurements intrinsic.

2.2.1 · Covectors

A covector is a linear functional on a tangent space. Differentials of scalar functions provide canonical examples.

2.2.2 · Cotangent bundles

The cotangent bundle assembles all cotangent spaces into a smooth bundle. It is a natural setting for differential forms and Hamiltonian geometry.

2.2.3 · Pullbacks

Pullback transports covectors and forms opposite to the direction of a smooth map. It requires no inverse map and respects composition.

3 · Tensor fields and differential forms

Tensor fields combine tangent and cotangent factors to describe geometric quantities. Differential forms are alternating covariant tensor fields.

3.1 · Tensor algebra

Tensor fields combine tangent and cotangent factors to describe geometric quantities. Their coordinate rules preserve the underlying intrinsic object.

3.1.1 · Tensor bundles

Tensor bundles collect multilinear objects of a fixed covariant and contravariant type. Sections of these bundles are tensor fields.

3.1.2 · Coordinate transformations

Tensor components transform using derivative matrices and their inverses as appropriate. These rules distinguish tensors from arbitrary arrays of functions.

3.1.3 · Contractions

Contraction pairs a tangent and cotangent factor to reduce tensor order. It produces invariant constructions such as traces.

3.2 · Differential forms

Differential forms are alternating covariant tensor fields. Their algebra and derivative unify many constructions in calculus.

3.2.1 · Wedge product

The wedge product combines forms while enforcing alternating signs. Its degree bookkeeping encodes oriented dimensional information.

3.2.2 · Exterior derivative

The exterior derivative differentiates forms and satisfies a graded product rule. Applying it twice gives zero.

3.2.3 · Pullback naturality

Pullback commutes with exterior differentiation for smooth maps. This identity makes differential equations on forms compatible with coordinate changes.

Part II · Central Theory and Techniques

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

4 · Integration and Stokes theorem

Orientation fixes the signs needed for integration on manifolds. Generalized Stokes relates an interior exterior derivative to a boundary integral.

4.1 · Orientations

Orientation fixes the signs needed for integration on manifolds. Boundary orientation must be consistent with the interior convention.

4.1.1 · Orientation forms

A nowhere-zero top-degree form can specify an orientation when such a form exists. Positive coordinate charts represent the same choice.

4.1.2 · Boundaries

A manifold with boundary has local half-space charts near boundary points. Its boundary inherits the orientation used in Stokes' theorem.

4.1.3 · Integration

Top-degree forms are integrated by local coordinates and a compatible assembly procedure. The transformation law makes the resulting integral intrinsic.

4.2 · Generalized Stokes

Generalized Stokes relates an interior exterior derivative to a boundary integral. It is the principal theorem linking differentiation and integration on manifolds.

4.2.1 · Partitions of unity

Partitions of unity decompose global data into chart-supported pieces. They permit a global theorem to be reduced to local Euclidean calculations.

4.2.2 · Theorem statement

For an oriented manifold with boundary, Stokes integrates a compactly supported form's derivative as its boundary integral. Compactness or support assumptions ensure the integrals are defined.

4.2.3 · Classical identities

The fundamental theorem, Green's theorem, and divergence and curl identities arise as special cases. Recovering them checks signs and dimensions.

5 · Vector fields, flows, and distributions

Vector fields generate local motions by ordinary differential equations. A distribution of tangent subspaces may or may not arise from tangent spaces to submanifolds.

5.1 · Flows

Vector fields generate local motions by ordinary differential equations. Flow-based differentiation extends this motion to geometric objects.

5.1.1 · Existence and uniqueness

Local existence and uniqueness of integral curves follow from suitable smoothness of the vector field. They make the associated motion deterministic near each initial point.

5.1.2 · Local flows

A local flow evolves points for the times where their integral curves exist. Completeness is the extra condition needed for all-time flow.

5.1.3 · Lie derivative

The Lie derivative differentiates a tensor field along a flow. It measures change using the flow's own geometric transport.

5.2 · Integrability

A distribution of tangent subspaces may or may not arise from tangent spaces to submanifolds. Lie brackets provide the local compatibility test.

5.2.1 · Lie brackets

The Lie bracket measures the failure of vector-field flows to commute infinitesimally. It tests whether directions generated by a distribution remain inside it.

5.2.2 · Frobenius theorem

Frobenius relates involutivity to local integrability for smooth constant-rank distributions. The constant-rank assumption is essential to this standard form.

5.2.3 · Foliations

A foliation decomposes a manifold locally into compatible lower-dimensional leaves. Integrable distributions provide their tangent directions.

6 · Riemannian geometry

A Riemannian metric equips each tangent space with a smoothly varying inner product. The Levi-Civita connection is the unique connection compatible with the metric and free of torsion.

6.1 · Metrics and lengths

A Riemannian metric equips each tangent space with a smoothly varying inner product. It defines lengths, angles, and volume.

6.1.1 · Riemannian metrics

A Riemannian metric is a positive definite symmetric covariant tensor field. It adds metric geometry to the smooth structure.

6.1.2 · Volume

The Riemannian volume element uses the determinant of the metric in coordinates. It defines a canonical measure, with a volume form when orientation is chosen.

6.1.3 · Induced structures

Submanifolds inherit a metric by restricting the ambient metric to tangent directions. This allows intrinsic measurements inside an embedding.

6.2 · Levi-Civita connection

The Levi-Civita connection is the unique connection compatible with the metric and free of torsion. It provides the intrinsic differentiation used in Riemannian geometry.

6.2.1 · Metric compatibility

Metric compatibility makes differentiation obey the inner-product product rule. Parallel transport consequently preserves lengths and angles.

6.2.2 · Torsion-free property

Zero torsion relates covariant derivatives in exchanged directions to the Lie bracket. Together with metric compatibility, it determines the connection.

6.2.3 · Coordinate formula

Christoffel symbols express the Levi-Civita connection in local coordinates. Their metric-derivative formula supports explicit calculations but the symbols are not tensor components.

7 · Geodesics and completeness

Geodesics are curves whose velocity is parallel along the curve. Completeness asks whether intrinsic motion and metric limits remain inside the manifold.

7.1 · Geodesic equation

Geodesics are curves whose velocity is parallel along the curve. Their differential equation defines an intrinsic notion of straight motion.

7.1.1 · Geodesic flow

Geodesic flow evolves initial positions and velocities on the tangent bundle. Local ODE theory establishes its existence and smooth dependence.

7.1.2 · Exponential map

The exponential map sends an initial tangent vector to the corresponding time-one geodesic point where defined. Near the origin, it provides useful local coordinates.

7.1.3 · Normal neighborhoods

A normal neighborhood is obtained from a region where the exponential map is a diffeomorphism. It supports radial geodesic descriptions and local distance arguments.

7.2 · Completeness

Completeness asks whether intrinsic motion and metric limits remain inside the manifold. Hopf–Rinow connects several equivalent forms in connected finite-dimensional Riemannian geometry.

7.2.1 · Hopf–Rinow theorem

Hopf–Rinow relates metric completeness, geodesic completeness, and compactness of closed bounded sets in its standard setting. It also guarantees minimizing geodesics between points.

7.2.2 · Minimizing geodesics

A minimizing geodesic attains the distance between its endpoints. Geodesics need not remain minimizing beyond the relevant local or global range.

7.2.3 · Metric completeness

Metric completeness requires every distance-Cauchy sequence to converge in the manifold. It excludes finite-distance escape through missing points.

8 · Curvature

Curvature measures the failure of covariant derivatives to commute. Model spaces calibrate curvature calculations and comparison statements.

8.1 · Curvature tensor

Curvature measures the failure of covariant derivatives to commute. Its contractions and sectional values encode different geometric information.

8.1.1 · Definitions and symmetries

The Riemann curvature tensor is defined from the connection and Lie bracket. Its symmetries reduce the number of independent components.

8.1.2 · Sectional curvature

Sectional curvature assigns curvature to a tangent two-plane. It compares local geometry with spherical, Euclidean, or hyperbolic models.

8.1.3 · Ricci curvature

Ricci curvature contracts the curvature tensor to a symmetric two-tensor. It controls averaged directional curvature and appears in many global theorems.

8.2 · Model spaces and comparison

Model spaces calibrate curvature calculations and comparison statements. Variations of geodesics explain how curvature affects distance and focusing.

8.2.1 · Constant curvature

Constant-curvature spaces supply uniform local curvature models. Spherical, Euclidean, and hyperbolic geometries exemplify the three signs.

8.2.2 · Jacobi fields

Jacobi fields describe infinitesimal variations through geodesics. Their second-order equation includes curvature and detects conjugate points.

8.2.3 · Comparison themes

Comparison theorems bound geometric behavior using curvature inequalities. Their conclusions depend on the particular curvature, completeness, and boundary assumptions.

Part III · Further Theory and Applications

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

9 · Submanifold geometry

Submanifold geometry separates ambient differentiation into tangent and normal components. Gauss–Codazzi relates a submanifold's intrinsic curvature to its extrinsic data.

9.1 · Second fundamental form

Submanifold geometry separates ambient differentiation into tangent and normal components. The second fundamental form measures the extrinsic bending.

9.1.1 · Shape operator

The shape operator describes variation of a chosen unit normal along a hypersurface. It is related to the second fundamental form through the induced metric.

9.1.2 · Normal connection

The normal connection differentiates normal fields using the normal component of the ambient connection. It becomes important in higher codimension.

9.1.3 · Principal curvatures

Principal curvatures are eigenvalues of the shape operator for an oriented hypersurface. They describe the main directional bending rates.

9.2 · Gauss–Codazzi equations

Gauss–Codazzi relates a submanifold's intrinsic curvature to its extrinsic data. These identities constrain which geometric data can come from an embedding.

9.2.1 · Intrinsic versus extrinsic curvature

Intrinsic curvature is determined by the induced metric, while extrinsic curvature depends on the embedding. The Gauss equation links them through the second fundamental form.

9.2.2 · Hypersurface examples

Spheres, planes, and cylinders distinguish intrinsic flatness from extrinsic bending. Their explicit formulas illustrate the Gauss and Codazzi relations.

9.2.3 · Rigidity themes

Rigidity asks when geometric data determine an embedding or metric up to an appropriate equivalence. Compatibility equations are starting constraints for such results.

10 · Global geometry and extensions

Gauss–Bonnet connects a curvature integral with a topological invariant. Further directions add symmetry, symplectic structure, or analytic equations to smooth geometry.

10.1 · Gauss–Bonnet

Gauss–Bonnet connects a curvature integral with a topological invariant. For surfaces it provides a striking bridge between local metric geometry and global topology.

10.1.1 · Curvature integrals

The integral of Gaussian curvature records accumulated intrinsic bending on a surface. Boundary versions add geodesic-curvature and corner contributions.

10.1.2 · Euler characteristic

Euler characteristic can be computed from a cell decomposition or homology. Gauss–Bonnet identifies it through curvature for appropriate surfaces.

10.1.3 · Surfaces

Closed oriented surfaces provide the standard global Gauss–Bonnet setting. Their genus constrains total curvature independently of the particular metric.

10.2 · Further subjects

Further directions add symmetry, symplectic structure, or analytic equations to smooth geometry. Each reuses the manifold and tensor language developed here.

10.2.1 · Lie groups

Lie groups combine smooth geometry with group operations. Their invariant vector fields produce Lie algebras and geometric symmetries.

10.2.2 · Symplectic geometry

A symplectic structure is a closed nondegenerate two-form. It provides the geometric framework for Hamiltonian dynamics.

10.2.3 · Gauge theory or geometric analysis

Gauge theory studies connections and their transformations, while geometric analysis studies geometric problems through differential equations. Both depend on careful interaction between topology, geometry, and analysis.
