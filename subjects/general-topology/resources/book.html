Part I · Foundations and Core Language

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

1 · Topological spaces and continuous maps

A topology specifies open sets without requiring a distance function. Continuity is expressed by inverse images of open sets.

1.1 · Topology axioms

A topology specifies open sets without requiring a distance function. Its axioms retain the set operations needed to discuss continuity and convergence.

1.1.1 · Open and closed sets

Open sets belong to the topology, and closed sets are their complements. These classes support neighborhood, closure, and boundary definitions.

1.1.2 · Bases and subbases

A basis generates open sets by unions, while a subbasis first allows finite intersections. Both give economical descriptions of a topology.

1.1.3 · Generated topologies

A generated topology is the smallest topology containing specified initial sets. This minimality supplies useful comparison and continuity properties.

1.2 · Continuity

Continuity is expressed by inverse images of open sets. Homeomorphisms identify spaces with exactly the same topological structure.

1.2.1 · Preimages

A map is continuous when inverse images of open target sets are open. This definition avoids any reliance on coordinates or metrics.

1.2.2 · Homeomorphisms

A homeomorphism is a continuous bijection with continuous inverse. A continuous bijection alone need not be a homeomorphism.

1.2.3 · Comparison with metric continuity

For metric spaces, the open-set definition agrees with the usual epsilon-delta condition. This verifies that topology extends familiar analysis.

2 · Subspaces and universal constructions

The subspace topology gives a subset the open sets inherited from its ambient space. Products and disjoint unions assemble spaces with prescribed mapping properties.

2.1 · Subspaces

The subspace topology gives a subset the open sets inherited from its ambient space. Relative closure and interior must be interpreted in that setting.

2.1.1 · Induced topology

The induced topology consists of intersections of ambient open sets with the subset. It makes the inclusion map continuous in a universal way.

2.1.2 · Closure and interior

Closure collects points inseparable from a set by neighborhoods, while interior collects contained open neighborhoods. Their values can change when the ambient space changes.

2.1.3 · Subspace examples

Intervals and dense subsets illustrate the difference between relative and ambient openness. Such examples prevent incorrect transfer of topological properties.

2.2 · Products and coproducts

Products and disjoint unions assemble spaces with prescribed mapping properties. The box topology shows that seemingly natural alternatives can behave differently.

2.2.1 · Product topology

The product topology is generated by conditions on finitely many coordinates. It is the weakest topology making every projection continuous.

2.2.2 · Box topology

The box topology permits independent open restrictions on all coordinates at once. For infinite products it can be strictly finer than the product topology.

2.2.3 · Disjoint unions

The disjoint-union topology makes a set open exactly when its intersection with each component is open. It is the categorical coproduct of spaces.

3 · Quotients and identifications

Quotient topology turns identifications into a topological space. Identification spaces create familiar geometric objects and unfamiliar pathologies.

3.1 · Quotient topology

Quotient topology turns identifications into a topological space. Its defining property controls maps defined on equivalence classes.

3.1.1 · Saturated sets

A saturated subset is a union of complete equivalence classes. Such sets are useful for understanding preimages under the quotient map.

3.1.2 · Quotient maps

A quotient map is a surjection whose target openness is exactly detected by inverse images. This is stronger than being a continuous surjection.

3.1.3 · Universal property

A map out of a quotient is continuous when its composite with the quotient map is continuous. This universal property is the main construction tool.

3.2 · Applications

Identification spaces create familiar geometric objects and unfamiliar pathologies. The examples expose which separation properties survive the construction.

3.2.1 · Identifying boundaries

Gluing boundary points or faces constructs circles, tori, and other spaces. The quotient topology determines how neighborhoods fit across the identifications.

3.2.2 · Orbit spaces

An orbit space identifies points related by a group action. Its topology records the geometry remaining after symmetry is removed.

3.2.3 · Examples of non-Hausdorff quotients

Quotients can fail to be Hausdorff even when the original space is well behaved. Explicit examples show why closed-orbit or stronger hypotheses may be needed.

Part II · Central Theory and Techniques

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

4 · Connectedness and compactness

Connectedness excludes decompositions into separated nonempty open pieces. Compactness abstracts the ability to extract finite information from arbitrary open covers.

4.1 · Connectedness

Connectedness excludes decompositions into separated nonempty open pieces. Local and path versions refine this global property.

4.1.1 · Components

Connected components are maximal connected subsets. They partition a space but need not be open.

4.1.2 · Local connectedness

Local connectedness means that points have bases of connected neighborhoods. It improves the relation between components and open sets.

4.1.3 · Path connectedness

Path connectedness joins each pair of points by a continuous interval map. It implies connectedness, but the converse fails in general.

4.2 · Compactness

Compactness abstracts the ability to extract finite information from arbitrary open covers. In metric spaces it also has useful sequential characterizations.

4.2.1 · Open-cover definition

A compact space has a finite subcover for every open cover. This formulation applies even when sequences do not detect the topology.

4.2.2 · Sequential compactness in metric spaces

In metric spaces, compactness is equivalent to every sequence having a convergent subsequence. The equivalence should not be transferred to all topological spaces.

4.2.3 · Local compactness

Local compactness gives points compact neighborhoods under the adopted convention. It is important in compactification and function-space theory.

5 · Countability and separation

Countability axioms limit the size of neighborhood and cover data. Separation axioms specify how points and closed sets can be distinguished by neighborhoods.

5.1 · Countability axioms

Countability axioms limit the size of neighborhood and cover data. They determine when sequences or countable constructions suffice.

5.1.1 · First and second countability

First countability gives countable neighborhood bases at points, while second countability gives one countable basis for the whole space. The latter is stronger.

5.1.2 · Separability

Separability means that a countable subset is dense. Its relation to basis countability depends on the category of spaces.

5.1.3 · Lindelöf property

A Lindelöf space has a countable subcover for every open cover. This property weakens compactness while retaining useful covering control.

5.2 · Separation axioms

Separation axioms specify how points and closed sets can be distinguished by neighborhoods. Different theorems require different levels of separation.

5.2.1 · T1 and Hausdorff

T1 means singletons are closed, while Hausdorff means distinct points have disjoint neighborhoods. Hausdorff spaces have unique limits when limits are defined by nets.

5.2.2 · Regularity

Regularity separates a point from a disjoint closed set by neighborhoods. The convention concerning an additional T1 requirement should be fixed.

5.2.3 · Normality

Normality separates disjoint closed sets by disjoint neighborhoods. It supports continuous separation and extension theorems.

6 · Metrization and completeness

Metrization asks whether a topology arises from some metric. Completeness concerns the existence of limits of Cauchy sequences in a chosen metric.

6.1 · Metrizable spaces

Metrization asks whether a topology arises from some metric. Countability and separation conditions supply useful sufficient criteria.

6.1.1 · Compatible metrics

A compatible metric induces exactly the given topology. Several distinct metrics may be compatible without having the same completeness properties.

6.1.2 · Urysohn metrization theorem

Urysohn's metrization theorem gives metrizability for regular second-countable T1 spaces. It converts topological hypotheses into a distance representation.

6.1.3 · Examples

Standard manifolds provide metrizable examples under their usual countability assumptions. Nonmetrizable examples show which hypotheses cannot simply be dropped.

6.2 · Complete metric spaces

Completeness concerns the existence of limits of Cauchy sequences in a chosen metric. Baire category derives strong consequences from this condition.

6.2.1 · Completion

Completion embeds a metric space densely into a complete one. It is unique up to the isometry fixing the original space.

6.2.2 · Baire category theorem

The Baire category theorem prevents a nonempty complete metric space from being a countable union of nowhere-dense closed sets. Equivalent formulations concern intersections of dense open sets.

6.2.3 · Applications

Category arguments prove existence of typical objects and support functional-analysis theorems. Each application must identify a Baire space and the relevant open dense sets.

7 · Compactification and product theorems

Compactification places a space densely inside a compact space. Product compactness is a major theorem whose proof uses covering or convergence methods.

7.1 · Compactifications

Compactification places a space densely inside a compact space. Different constructions preserve different classes of continuous maps.

7.1.1 · One-point compactification

The one-point compactification adds a point at infinity to a suitable locally compact Hausdorff noncompact space. Its neighborhoods encode complements of compact sets.

7.1.2 · Stone–Čech idea

The Stone–Čech compactification of a completely regular Hausdorff space has a universal extension property for maps into compact Hausdorff spaces. It generally adds much more than one point.

7.1.3 · Examples

The real line and discrete spaces illustrate different compactification behaviors. Comparing them clarifies how neighborhoods at added points are determined.

7.2 · Product compactness

Product compactness is a major theorem whose proof uses covering or convergence methods. Its relation to choice reveals a foundational dimension of topology.

7.2.1 · Tychonoff theorem

Tychonoff's theorem states that arbitrary products of compact spaces are compact in the product topology. Finite-coordinate neighborhoods are essential to that topology.

7.2.2 · Alexander subbase theorem

Alexander's subbase theorem reduces compactness checks to covers from a subbasis under the usual choice setting. It is an efficient route to product compactness.

7.2.3 · Choice principles

Some general compactness principles depend on choice principles. Their exact logical strength depends on the class of spaces and the formulation used.

8 · Separation and extension theorems

Urysohn's lemma replaces neighborhood separation with a continuous scalar function. Tietze extends real-valued continuous functions from closed subsets of normal spaces.

8.1 · Urysohn lemma

Urysohn's lemma replaces neighborhood separation with a continuous scalar function. It is a key consequence of normality.

8.1.1 · Normal spaces

Normal spaces separate disjoint closed sets by disjoint open neighborhoods. This is the hypothesis behind the standard continuous-separation construction.

8.1.2 · Construction

The construction nests open neighborhoods indexed by dyadic rationals. Taking an infimum produces a continuous function with prescribed endpoint values.

8.1.3 · Consequences

Continuous separation supports embeddings, extension theorems, and metrization. It turns qualitative topology into numerical measurements.

8.2 · Tietze extension

Tietze extends real-valued continuous functions from closed subsets of normal spaces. It strengthens separation into a general extension procedure.

8.2.1 · Extension of real-valued functions

Under the standard normal-space hypotheses, a continuous real-valued function on a closed subset extends continuously to the space. Bounded versions preserve a chosen interval range.

8.2.2 · Partitions of unity preview

Partitions of unity assemble local data using continuous weights that sum to one. Their existence in useful forms requires additional covering hypotheses such as paracompactness.

9 · Function spaces and topological groups

Topologies on function spaces determine what convergence of maps means. A topological group combines algebraic operations with continuity.

9.1 · Function-space topologies

Topologies on function spaces determine what convergence of maps means. Compact-open and uniform topologies serve different purposes.

9.1.1 · Compact-open topology

The compact-open topology controls images of compact subsets inside target open sets. It is a natural topology for continuous mapping spaces.

9.1.2 · Uniform convergence

Uniform convergence bounds the pointwise discrepancy over the entire domain using a suitable target structure. It is stronger than convergence on each compact subset in general.

9.1.3 · Evaluation maps

Evaluation sends a function and a point to the function value. Its continuity depends on the function-space topology and domain hypotheses.

9.2 · Topological groups

A topological group combines algebraic operations with continuity. Translation symmetry makes its local and global topology closely related.

9.2.1 · Continuity of operations

Multiplication and inversion must be continuous in a topological group. Translations are then homeomorphisms and transport neighborhoods of the identity.

9.2.2 · Quotient groups

Quotient groups by normal subgroups inherit a quotient topology. Closedness of the subgroup is important for Hausdorff quotient behavior.

9.2.3 · Compact groups

Compact groups combine compact topology with continuous algebraic symmetry. They provide a bridge to Haar measure and representation theory.

10 · Dimension, uniformity, and further topics

Dimension theory measures topological complexity without assuming coordinates. Uniform structures retain a notion of uniformly close pairs without specifying a metric.

10.1 · Dimension theory

Dimension theory measures topological complexity without assuming coordinates. Its definitions must be distinguished from algebraic or metric notions of dimension.

10.1.1 · Covering dimension

Covering dimension bounds the overlap multiplicity needed in refinements of open covers. It is a topological invariant under the appropriate definition.

10.1.2 · Examples

Euclidean spaces and totally disconnected compact examples calibrate covering dimension. Carefully chosen examples distinguish it from other dimension notions.

10.1.3 · Invariance questions

Dimension invariance asks what constructions or equivalences preserve dimension. Such questions prevent coordinates from being mistaken for intrinsic structure.

10.2 · Uniform structures

Uniform structures retain a notion of uniformly close pairs without specifying a metric. They extend uniform continuity and completeness beyond metric spaces.

10.2.1 · Uniform spaces

A uniform space is described by entourages of the diagonal satisfying compatibility axioms. These encode a uniform notion of closeness.

10.2.2 · Completion

Uniform completion adds limits of appropriate Cauchy filters or nets under separation assumptions. It extends metric completion to the uniform setting.

10.2.3 · Advanced routes and applications

Uniform spaces connect function spaces, topological groups, and compactification. Further study should specify which additional structures each application uses.
