Part I · Foundations and Core Language

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

1 · Vector spaces and linear maps

Subspaces and quotients organize linear constraints and identifications. Duality studies a vector space through linear scalar-valued measurements.

1.1 · Subspaces and quotients

Subspaces and quotients organize linear constraints and identifications. Dimension provides a numerical invariant for the resulting constructions.

1.1.1 · Span, independence, dimension

Spanning and linear independence characterize bases. Dimension counts basis vectors and measures the number of independent coordinates.

1.1.2 · Quotient spaces

A quotient space identifies vectors differing by a chosen subspace. It records the directions that remain after those identifications.

1.1.3 · Rank-nullity

Rank-nullity adds the dimensions of kernel and image to recover the domain dimension. It quantifies the balance between constraints and freedom.

1.2 · Duality

Duality studies a vector space through linear scalar-valued measurements. It provides a coordinate-independent framework for equations and transpose maps.

1.2.1 · Dual spaces

The dual space consists of linear functionals on the original space. In finite dimensions, a basis determines a corresponding dual basis.

1.2.2 · Annihilators

The annihilator of a subspace contains functionals vanishing on that subspace. It converts subspace inclusions into reversed inclusions in the dual.

1.2.3 · Transpose and dual maps

A dual map acts on functionals by composition with the original map. Its matrix is the transpose in the corresponding dual bases.

2 · Polynomial structure of operators

Invariant subspaces isolate parts of an operator's action. Two associated polynomials summarize important operator information.

2.1 · Invariant subspaces

Invariant subspaces isolate parts of an operator's action. Cyclic structure turns this action into polynomial algebra.

2.1.1 · Cyclic vectors

A cyclic vector generates its invariant subspace by repeated application of the operator. It leads to a companion-matrix description.

2.1.2 · Annihilators

Here annihilators are polynomials that act as zero on vectors or modules. Their ideals encode the relations satisfied by the operator action.

2.1.3 · Invariant factors

Invariant factors are ordered polynomial divisors describing cyclic module components. They classify similarity without requiring all eigenvalues to lie in the field.

2.2 · Minimal and characteristic polynomials

Two associated polynomials summarize important operator information. Their factorization controls invariant decompositions and functional calculus.

2.2.1 · Cayley–Hamilton

Cayley–Hamilton states that an operator satisfies its characteristic polynomial. This yields a universal polynomial relation for finite-dimensional operators.

2.2.2 · Polynomial functional calculus

Polynomial functional calculus evaluates polynomials at an operator. It turns scalar algebraic identities into operator identities.

2.2.3 · Primary decomposition

Primary decomposition separates the space according to relatively prime factors of an annihilating polynomial. Each component captures one irreducible polynomial type.

3 · Canonical forms

Rational canonical form classifies operators over an arbitrary field. Jordan form refines operator structure when the characteristic polynomial splits.

3.1 · Rational canonical form

Rational canonical form classifies operators over an arbitrary field. It uses polynomial invariant factors rather than a choice of eigenvectors.

3.1.1 · Companion matrices

A companion matrix represents multiplication by the variable on a cyclic polynomial quotient. It is the basic block of rational canonical form.

3.1.2 · Invariant factors

Invariant factors satisfy successive divisibility relations. Their list uniquely determines the rational canonical block structure.

3.1.3 · Similarity classification

Two matrices are similar exactly when their rational canonical data agree. This expresses operator equivalence independently of coordinates.

3.2 · Jordan form

Jordan form refines operator structure when the characteristic polynomial splits. Its blocks record generalized eigenvectors and nilpotent behavior.

3.2.1 · Generalized eigenspaces

A generalized eigenspace is the kernel of a sufficiently high power of the shifted operator. These spaces assemble the full space when the polynomial splits.

3.2.2 · Nilpotent operators

A nilpotent operator has some power equal to zero. The dimensions of its successive kernels determine its Jordan blocks.

3.2.3 · Algebraic multiplicities

Algebraic multiplicity is the multiplicity of an eigenvalue in the characteristic polynomial. Comparing it with eigenspace dimension detects failures of diagonalizability.

Part II · Central Theory and Techniques

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

4 · Modules over principal ideal domains

A linear operator equips its vector space with a polynomial-module structure. The structure theorem classifies finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain.

4.1 · Module actions

A linear operator equips its vector space with a polynomial-module structure. This translates invariant subspaces and decompositions into module language.

4.1.1 · Linear operators as F[x]-modules

Letting the polynomial variable act as the operator makes the space an F[x]-module. Polynomial relations then become module annihilation relations.

4.1.2 · Submodules

Submodules for this polynomial action are precisely invariant linear subspaces. The correspondence allows module theorems to study operators.

4.1.3 · Cyclic decomposition

Cyclic decomposition writes the polynomial module as a direct sum of cyclic pieces. Those pieces produce canonical matrix blocks.

4.2 · Structure theorem

The structure theorem classifies finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain. Operator canonical forms are a major specialization.

4.2.1 · Finitely generated modules

Finite generation means that finitely many elements generate the module using ring coefficients. Over a PID, it permits a precise free-plus-torsion decomposition.

4.2.2 · Invariant-factor form

Invariant-factor form organizes the torsion summands with a divisibility chain. The resulting list is unique up to associates.

4.2.3 · Classification applications

Applying the theorem to integers classifies finitely generated abelian groups. Applying it to polynomial rings classifies finite-dimensional linear operators.

5 · Bilinear and quadratic forms

Bilinear forms assign a scalar to pairs of vectors linearly in each input. Quadratic forms encode degree-two scalar expressions on vector spaces.

5.1 · Bilinear forms

Bilinear forms assign a scalar to pairs of vectors linearly in each input. Changes of basis act by congruence rather than similarity.

5.1.1 · Matrices and congruence

A matrix represents a bilinear form after choosing a basis. Replacing the basis transforms that matrix by congruence.

5.1.2 · Orthogonality

Orthogonality means vanishing of the bilinear pairing for a pair of vectors. It need not arise from a positive inner product.

5.1.3 · Nondegeneracy

A nondegenerate form has trivial radical. This means that pairing against all vectors detects every nonzero vector.

5.2 · Quadratic forms

Quadratic forms encode degree-two scalar expressions on vector spaces. Their classification depends strongly on the field and its characteristic.

5.2.1 · Classification over R and C

Over the reals, signs distinguish nondegenerate quadratic forms, while over the complexes rank suffices in characteristic zero. These classifications follow from congruence reductions.

5.2.2 · Signature

Signature counts positive and negative directions in a real symmetric form. Sylvester's law makes these counts independent of basis.

5.2.3 · Witt decomposition motivation

Witt decomposition separates hyperbolic behavior from an anisotropic part under appropriate characteristic assumptions. It prepares the arithmetic theory of forms.

6 · Inner-product spaces and spectral theory

Inner products identify linear maps with adjoints and subspaces with orthogonal complements. Spectral theorems describe operators by orthogonal eigenvectors under symmetry assumptions.

6.1 · Adjoints

Inner products identify linear maps with adjoints and subspaces with orthogonal complements. These structures make symmetry of an operator precise.

6.1.1 · Orthogonal complements

An orthogonal complement consists of vectors perpendicular to a given subspace. In finite-dimensional inner-product spaces, it gives a direct-sum complement.

6.1.2 · Adjoint operators

The adjoint is defined by moving an operator across the inner product. It generalizes conjugate transpose independently of coordinates.

6.1.3 · Normality

An operator is normal when it commutes with its adjoint. In complex finite dimensions, normality characterizes unitary diagonalizability.

6.2 · Spectral theorems

Spectral theorems describe operators by orthogonal eigenvectors under symmetry assumptions. Variational characterizations connect eigenvalues to optimization.

6.2.1 · Real symmetric and Hermitian matrices

Real symmetric and complex Hermitian matrices have real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenbases. Their quadratic forms are therefore directly readable from spectral data.

6.2.2 · Unitary diagonalization

Unitary diagonalization uses an orthonormal complex basis to diagonalize a normal matrix. It preserves the inner-product geometry of the original coordinates.

6.2.3 · Variational principles

Min-max principles characterize eigenvalues through extrema of Rayleigh quotients on subspaces. They support comparison and perturbation bounds.

7 · Tensor and exterior algebra

Tensor products linearize bilinear maps. Exterior and symmetric powers impose antisymmetry or symmetry on tensors.

7.1 · Tensor products

Tensor products linearize bilinear maps. Their universal property separates the construction from a particular coordinate representation.

7.1.1 · Universal property

Every bilinear map factors uniquely through a linear map on the tensor product. This property defines the tensor product up to canonical isomorphism.

7.1.2 · Bases

Tensor products of basis vectors form a basis in finite dimensions. Their indexing explains the product rule for dimensions.

7.1.3 · Tensor maps

Linear maps on factors induce a linear map on their tensor product. This construction transports algebraic operations to multilinear settings.

7.2 · Exterior and symmetric powers

Exterior and symmetric powers impose antisymmetry or symmetry on tensors. They organize determinants and polynomial constructions.

7.2.1 · Alternating forms

Alternating forms vanish when two arguments coincide and change sign under swaps. They naturally pair with exterior powers.

7.2.2 · Determinants

The top exterior power turns a linear map into multiplication by its determinant. This gives an intrinsic interpretation of determinant formulas.

7.2.3 · Multilinear constructions

Symmetric and exterior constructions encode polynomial and alternating data. Their functorial behavior makes them useful across algebra and geometry.

8 · Matrix analysis

Positive semidefinite matrices order quadratic forms and support useful factorizations. Singular values measure amplification without requiring square or normal matrices.

8.1 · Positive matrices

Positive semidefinite matrices order quadratic forms and support useful factorizations. Their spectral structure leads to canonical roots and stable computations.

8.1.1 · Positive semidefinite order

The semidefinite order compares Hermitian matrices by positivity of their difference. It compares the corresponding quadratic forms in every direction.

8.1.2 · Square roots

A positive semidefinite matrix has a unique positive semidefinite square root. Spectral decomposition constructs it explicitly.

8.1.3 · Cholesky factorization

A positive definite matrix admits a Cholesky factorization into a triangular factor and its adjoint. This is efficient for linear systems and quadratic forms.

8.2 · Singular values

Singular values measure amplification without requiring square or normal matrices. They connect geometry, factorization, and approximation.

8.2.1 · SVD

The singular value decomposition factors a matrix using orthonormal input and output directions. Its nonnegative singular values quantify directional stretching.

8.2.2 · Polar decomposition

Polar decomposition writes a matrix as a positive factor and a unitary or partial-isometry factor. It separates stretching from isometric motion.

8.2.3 · Low-rank approximation

Truncated SVD gives optimal low-rank approximations in standard spectral and Frobenius norms. It provides a rigorous basis for compression.

Part III · Further Theory and Applications

9 · Perturbation and numerical structure

Spectral data can change when matrix entries are perturbed. Numerical methods should preserve useful matrix structure while controlling rounding effects.

9.1 · Eigenvalue sensitivity

Spectral data can change when matrix entries are perturbed. Sensitivity analysis identifies stable quantities and difficult cases.

9.1.1 · Rayleigh quotient

The Rayleigh quotient is a normalized quadratic form. For Hermitian matrices, its extrema give extreme eigenvalues.

9.1.2 · Perturbation bounds

Perturbation bounds control changes in eigenvalues or invariant subspaces under matrix errors. Their strength depends on symmetry and spectral gaps.

9.1.3 · Conditioning

Conditioning measures how strongly the exact answer responds to input perturbations. It is a property of the problem rather than of a chosen algorithm.

9.2 · Stable computation

Numerical methods should preserve useful matrix structure while controlling rounding effects. Backward analysis separates algorithmic error from intrinsic conditioning.

9.2.1 · QR and related factorizations

QR factorization splits a matrix into orthogonal and triangular factors. It supports least-squares and eigenvalue algorithms without unnecessary squaring of condition numbers.

9.2.2 · Backward error

Backward error asks whether a computed answer exactly solves a nearby problem. A small backward error is useful when the original problem is also well conditioned.

9.2.3 · Iterative methods

Iterative methods improve approximate solutions through repeated operator applications. Their convergence depends on spectral structure and appropriate stopping tests.

10 · Applications and synthesis

Least squares and inverse problems estimate unknown vectors from incomplete or inconsistent equations. Spectral ideas support geometric, dynamical, and physical applications.

10.1 · Least squares and inverse problems

Least squares and inverse problems estimate unknown vectors from incomplete or inconsistent equations. Factorizations and regularization control nonuniqueness and instability.

10.1.1 · Normal equations

Normal equations characterize least-squares stationarity by orthogonality of the residual to the column space. Directly forming them can worsen numerical conditioning.

10.1.2 · Pseudoinverse

The Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse selects minimum-norm least-squares solutions. SVD provides a constructive formula.

10.1.3 · Regularization

Regularization modifies an unstable inverse problem by penalizing or restricting undesirable solutions. The regularization parameter balances data fit against stability.

10.2 · Spectral applications

Spectral ideas support geometric, dynamical, and physical applications. The examples show how abstract decompositions become practical reasoning tools.

10.2.1 · Graphs

Spectral graph methods encode connectivity and diffusion through adjacency or Laplacian matrices. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors reveal structural properties of the graph.

10.2.2 · Differential equations

Matrix exponentials solve constant-coefficient linear differential systems. Canonical and spectral forms explain growth and repeated-eigenvalue effects.

10.2.3 · Quantum observables and state spaces

In finite-dimensional quantum models, Hermitian operators represent observables and positive trace-one operators represent mixed states. Spectral decomposition connects measurement values with the geometry of state space.
