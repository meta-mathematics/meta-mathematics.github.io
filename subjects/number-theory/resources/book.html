Part I · Foundations and Core Language

This part groups the following chapters of the annotated graduate book. Its arrows express book organization, not proof prerequisites.

1 · Arithmetic foundations

This chapter establishes the elementary language used throughout number theory. Divisibility, congruences, and arithmetic functions provide the first tools for turning integer questions into structured arguments.

1.1 · Divisibility and gcd

Divisibility organizes the integers through exact factor relations, while the greatest common divisor captures the shared arithmetic structure of two integers. The section develops these ideas into reusable tools for equations, congruences, and factorization.

1.1.1 · Euclidean division

The division algorithm writes an integer as a quotient times a divisor plus a controlled remainder. Its repeated application gives the Euclidean algorithm, a finite procedure for computing greatest common divisors.

1.1.2 · Bézout identities

Bézout’s identity expresses the greatest common divisor as an integer linear combination of the original integers. This characterization proves useful for testing coprimality, solving linear Diophantine equations, and constructing modular inverses.

1.1.3 · Prime valuations

The valuation at a prime records how many times that prime occurs in an integer’s factorization. Valuations turn divisibility questions into comparisons of exponents and foreshadow local methods used later in the book.

1.2 · Congruences

Congruence arithmetic studies integers after identifying numbers that differ by a multiple of a fixed modulus. It provides a finite setting in which divisibility, equations, and multiplicative structure can be analyzed.

1.2.1 · Residue classes

Residue classes collect integers with the same remainder modulo a fixed positive integer. Addition and multiplication of classes make the quotient ring a natural environment for modular equations.

1.2.2 · Chinese remainder theorem

The Chinese remainder theorem combines compatible congruences with coprime moduli into a unique residue class modulo their product. It decomposes many calculations modulo a composite integer into independent calculations at smaller moduli.

1.2.3 · Orders modulo n

The multiplicative order of a unit is the smallest positive exponent that returns it to the identity modulo n. Orders describe cyclic behavior and help determine when powers generate all units.

2 · Multiplicative arithmetic

This chapter studies arithmetic functions and the multiplicative groups of residue classes. These structures turn integer factorization into formulas and organize power behavior modulo n.

2.1 · Arithmetic functions

Arithmetic functions assign numerical data to positive integers and often reflect their prime factorization. Multiplicativity makes it possible to compute global values from prime-power cases.

2.1.1 · Multiplicativity

A function is multiplicative when its value on a product of coprime integers factors into the corresponding values. This property reduces many identities to prime powers and supports Euler products later.

2.1.2 · Divisor sums

Summing a function over the divisors of an integer produces a new arithmetic function with useful convolution structure. The divisor-sum viewpoint links counting problems to factorization and average order.

2.1.3 · Möbius inversion

Möbius inversion reverses divisor-sum relations by using the alternating behavior of the Möbius function. It recovers an arithmetic function from its aggregate divisor data.

2.2 · Units and primitive roots

The invertible residue classes modulo n form a finite abelian group whose structure depends on n. This section explains when that group is cyclic and how primitive roots encode all its elements.

2.2.1 · Structure of the unit group modulo n

The group of units consists of residue classes that admit multiplicative inverses. Its decomposition reveals which orders can occur and whether a single element can generate the entire group.

2.2.2 · Cyclicity criteria

Cyclicity criteria identify the moduli for which the unit group is generated by one element. The classification connects prime-power behavior with the Chinese remainder theorem.

2.2.3 · Primitive roots

A primitive root modulo n is a generator of the unit group when that group is cyclic. Such roots provide a discrete logarithm coordinate system for multiplicative congruences.

3 · Quadratic residues

Quadratic residue theory asks when a congruence has a square root and how that answer varies with the prime modulus. Its central reciprocity laws reveal surprising relations among different primes.

3.1 · Squares modulo primes

For an odd prime, nonzero residue classes split into quadratic residues and nonresidues. Symbols and counting arguments provide a compact language for deciding and comparing square classes.

3.1.1 · Legendre symbols

The Legendre symbol records whether an integer is a square modulo an odd prime, with signs distinguishing residues from nonresidues. Its multiplicative rules compress many separate congruence checks into one calculation.

3.1.2 · Euler criterion

Euler’s criterion computes the Legendre symbol by raising an integer to a power modulo the prime. It connects residue questions to the structure of the finite multiplicative group.

3.1.3 · Residue counting

Counting arguments establish how many nonzero squares occur modulo a prime and how residue classes pair under negation. These counts prepare the combinatorial intuition behind reciprocity.

3.2 · Quadratic reciprocity

Quadratic reciprocity compares the solvability of x² congruent to p modulo q with the reversed question modulo p. The section develops a proof route and records the supplementary cases needed for complete calculations.

3.2.1 · Gauss lemma

Gauss’s lemma converts the Legendre symbol into the parity of a count of least residues. It supplies a concrete bridge from modular multiplication to the reciprocity law.

3.2.2 · Reciprocity law

The quadratic reciprocity law relates two Legendre symbols for distinct odd primes, with a sign determined by their residue classes modulo four. It is one of the central structural theorems of elementary number theory.

3.2.3 · Supplementary laws

The supplementary laws handle the special symbols involving −1 and 2. Together with quadratic reciprocity, they give a complete practical test for quadratic residuosity modulo an odd prime.

Part II · Central Theory and Techniques

This part groups the following chapters of the annotated graduate book. Its arrows express book organization, not proof prerequisites.

4 · Congruence methods

This chapter develops methods for solving congruences beyond direct computation. Lifting and local viewpoints explain how solutions modulo one prime power can persist or fail at higher precision.

4.1 · Lifting solutions

Lifting asks whether a solution modulo p can be extended to solutions modulo p², p³, and higher powers. The key theorem gives a precise derivative condition that guarantees a unique lift.

4.1.1 · Hensel lemma

Hensel’s lemma lifts a simple root of a polynomial modulo a prime to a root in the p-adic integers. It converts approximate solutions into exact local solutions when the derivative remains a unit.

4.1.2 · p-adic lifting

Repeated lifting produces compatible solutions at every power of p and hence a p-adic solution. The process explains why local arithmetic retains information invisible at any single finite modulus.

4.1.3 · Local solvability

Local solvability tests an integer equation over completions such as the real numbers and p-adic fields. Local obstructions can rule out global integer or rational solutions before a harder global argument begins.

4.2 · Exponential congruences

Exponential congruences involve unknowns or parameters in exponents, so ordinary additive methods are often insufficient. Group structure and discrete logarithms provide a systematic way to analyze their periodicity.

4.2.1 · Discrete logarithms

A discrete logarithm asks for an exponent that produces a given group element as a power of a chosen generator. Existence and uniqueness depend on the subgroup generated and its order.

4.2.2 · Orders and indices

The order of an element and its index in a finite group constrain which exponent congruences can be solved. These invariants reduce exponential equations to arithmetic in cyclic groups.

4.2.3 · Selected applications

Applications include power-residue tests and congruences involving repeated exponentiation. Each example emphasizes the need to identify the relevant group and verify that the base is a unit.

5 · Diophantine equations

Diophantine equations seek integer or rational solutions to polynomial relations. This chapter pairs classical families with proof techniques that exploit divisibility, descent, and local information.

5.1 · Classical equations

Classical equations illustrate how algebraic identities and arithmetic restrictions interact. Their solutions offer model examples for parametrization, recurrence, and obstruction arguments.

5.1.1 · Pythagorean triples

The equation x²+y²=z² has a complete parametrization when common factors and parity are handled carefully. The derivation illustrates primitive solutions and the role of coprimality.

5.1.2 · Pell equations

Pell’s equation x²−dy²=1 can have infinitely many integer solutions when d is a nonsquare positive integer. Continued fractions and units in quadratic fields explain how these solutions are generated.

5.1.3 · Sums of squares

Representing an integer as a sum of squares connects congruence restrictions with factorization and geometry. The section introduces standard criteria and clarifies which hypotheses depend on the number of squares.

5.2 · Methods of proof

The same equation may be attacked through descent, congruences, or local analysis, and each method exposes a different obstruction. Comparing methods helps identify what a proof establishes and where its limits lie.

5.2.1 · Infinite descent

Infinite descent assumes a solution exists and constructs a strictly smaller solution of the same kind. A well-founded size measure makes the resulting infinite chain impossible.

5.2.2 · Congruence obstructions

Congruence obstructions show that an equation has no solution in a residue class system modulo a chosen integer. A successful obstruction is decisive, while failure to find one proves nothing about global solvability.

5.2.3 · Local-to-global questions

Local-to-global principles ask whether solvability in every completion implies a global solution. The section introduces settings where the principle succeeds and counterexamples that show why it cannot be assumed universally.

6 · Algebraic number fields

Number fields extend the rational numbers by adjoining algebraic elements, creating a setting where many Diophantine equations become factorization problems. The chapter develops their integer rings and the invariants used to study them.

6.1 · Number fields and integers

A number field is a finite extension of the rationals, and its algebraic integers form a distinguished subring. Understanding this ring is essential because rational factorization alone may not capture the arithmetic of the field.

6.1.1 · Algebraic elements

An algebraic element satisfies a nonzero polynomial with rational coefficients, and its minimal polynomial records its algebraic relations. This definition provides the extension elements from which a number field is built.

6.1.2 · Rings of integers

The ring of integers consists of all elements of the number field that satisfy monic polynomials with integer coefficients. It is the natural domain for integral factorization and prime decomposition.

6.1.3 · Integral bases

An integral basis is a basis of the ring of integers as a free abelian group. Computing one turns field arithmetic into explicit integer-coordinate calculations.

6.2 · Embeddings and invariants

Embeddings place a number field inside the complex numbers in all possible ways compatible with its arithmetic. Norms, traces, and discriminants summarize how these embeddings interact and provide tools for bounding and classifying arithmetic objects.

6.2.1 · Norm and trace

The field norm and trace aggregate the conjugates of an element through products and sums. They connect field arithmetic to rational values and help test integrality and units.

6.2.2 · Discriminant

The discriminant measures linear dependence among embeddings of an integral basis and records ramification information. It also supplies quantitative bounds used in class-group calculations.

6.2.3 · Signatures and embeddings

Real and complex embeddings classify the archimedean places of a number field. Their counts determine the signature, which later controls the rank of the unit group.

7 · Ideals and factorization

Unique factorization of elements can fail in a number ring, but ideals restore a robust form of unique factorization. This chapter introduces Dedekind domains and measures the remaining failure through the ideal class group.

7.1 · Dedekind domains

Dedekind domains are Noetherian, integrally closed domains of dimension one, including rings of integers in number fields. Their prime ideals factor uniquely and provide the central algebraic setting for arithmetic.

7.1.1 · Prime ideals

Prime ideals generalize prime numbers as objects that detect divisibility and quotient domains. Their behavior under field extensions is one of the main subjects of algebraic number theory.

7.1.2 · Ideal factorization

Every nonzero ideal in a Dedekind domain factors uniquely as a product of prime ideals. This theorem replaces element factorization when irreducibles no longer behave uniquely.

7.1.3 · Localization

Localization focuses a ring near a chosen prime by making other elements invertible. It isolates local divisibility and helps explain how global ideal structure is assembled from prime behavior.

7.2 · Class groups

The class group records which ideals fail to be principal, and therefore measures the obstruction to unique factorization of elements. Geometric bounds make this group finite and computable in examples.

7.2.1 · Ideal classes

Two fractional ideals define the same class when their quotient is principal. The resulting group distinguishes domains with unique factorization from those where ideal factorization is strictly richer.

7.2.2 · Minkowski bounds

Minkowski’s theorem bounds the norm of an ideal representative in every ideal class. This converts an abstract finiteness theorem into a finite search for class groups.

7.2.3 · Examples of nonunique element factorization

Explicit number rings can contain elements with distinct irreducible factorizations, even though their ideals factor uniquely. Worked examples show exactly how nonprincipal ideals account for the discrepancy.

8 · Units, primes, and extensions

This chapter studies two kinds of arithmetic data in field extensions: invertible algebraic integers and the behavior of prime ideals. Together they describe the multiplicative structure and the splitting of primes across fields.

8.1 · Unit groups

Units are elements with multiplicative inverses inside the ring of integers. Their structure is finite torsion times a free abelian group, linking field embeddings to concrete solutions of norm equations.

8.1.1 · Dirichlet unit theorem

Dirichlet’s unit theorem gives the rank of the unit group in terms of the real and complex embeddings of the field. It is a structural result with both qualitative and computational consequences.

8.1.2 · Regulators

The regulator measures the size of a fundamental domain for the logarithms of units. It quantifies the spacing of independent units and enters class-number formulas.

8.1.3 · Examples in quadratic fields

Real quadratic fields have infinitely many units generated up to sign by a fundamental unit, while imaginary quadratic fields have only finitely many. Examples connect this contrast to Pell equations and embedding geometry.

8.2 · Prime behavior

A prime ideal in the base field may split, remain inert, or ramify in an extension. The decomposition and inertia groups organize these possibilities and connect field automorphisms to arithmetic.

8.2.1 · Ramification

Ramification measures the multiplicity with which a prime ideal appears after extension to a larger ring of integers. Discriminants detect ramified primes and constrain how extensions can behave.

8.2.2 · Decomposition and inertia

The decomposition group preserves a prime above a given base prime, while the inertia group measures the part acting trivially on the residue field. Their quotient describes the induced residue-field symmetry.

8.2.3 · Splitting in extensions

Splitting describes how a prime factors into primes in an extension, including the split, inert, and ramified cases. The pattern reflects the Galois group and is a gateway to class field theory.

Part III · Further Theory and Applications

9 · Analytic number theory

Analytic number theory studies arithmetic questions by encoding sequences and prime data in analytic functions. The chapter introduces Dirichlet series and outlines how character sums and nonvanishing lead to prime-distribution results.

9.1 · Dirichlet series

A Dirichlet series packages arithmetic coefficients into a complex function whose convergence reflects their growth. Euler products expose prime factorization when multiplicativity and convergence permit them.

9.1.1 · Convergence

Convergence regions depend on coefficient growth and determine where termwise operations are justified. Absolute convergence is especially important for rearranging sums and forming Euler products.

9.1.2 · Euler products

Euler products express suitable Dirichlet series as products over primes. They translate unique factorization into analytic structure and make the primes visible to complex analysis.

9.1.3 · Zeta functions

The Riemann zeta function and its number-field analogues encode counting information about integers and ideals. Their zeros, poles, and special values carry arithmetic consequences.

9.2 · Primes in progressions

Dirichlet’s theorem states that every reduced residue class contains infinitely many primes. Its proof architecture combines characters, L-functions, and a crucial nonvanishing argument at the boundary.

9.2.1 · Characters

Dirichlet characters are periodic, completely multiplicative functions adapted to a modulus. Orthogonality relations let them isolate a chosen residue class in a prime-counting sum.

9.2.2 · Nonvanishing ideas

Nonvanishing of Dirichlet L-functions at a key point prevents cancellation from destroying the main term. The section explains the logical role of this input without claiming a full analytic proof in the skeleton.

9.2.3 · Dirichlet theorem roadmap

The proof roadmap moves from character orthogonality to analytic properties of L-functions and then to asymptotic prime counts. Each dependency is identified so a later chapter expansion can supply precise estimates and hypotheses.

10 · Local and p-adic methods

Local methods study arithmetic through completions, especially the p-adic numbers, where divisibility by a prime determines a natural notion of distance. This chapter connects finite congruences to infinite local fields and local solvability.

10.1 · p-adic numbers

The p-adic numbers complete the rationals using the p-adic absolute value, in which numbers divisible by high powers of p are small. They provide a setting where congruence solutions can converge to exact roots.

10.1.1 · Completion

Completion adds limits of Cauchy sequences to a metric space, here using p-adic distance. The construction turns compatible residues modulo increasing powers of p into p-adic numbers.

10.1.2 · Ultrametric topology

The p-adic absolute value satisfies the stronger ultrametric triangle inequality, so nested balls have unusually rigid geometry. This topology explains convergence and compactness properties used in local arguments.

10.1.3 · Hensel’s lemma revisited

Hensel’s lemma is restated in the language of complete local fields and p-adic convergence. This viewpoint clarifies why a simple approximate root determines a nearby exact root.

10.2 · Local fields

A local field is a complete field with a locally compact topology, such as the real numbers or a finite extension of a p-adic field. Valuations, residue fields, and norms organize its arithmetic structure.

10.2.1 · Valuations

A valuation measures the order of vanishing or divisibility of a field element and induces an absolute value. It separates units from elements that are small in the local topology.

10.2.2 · Extensions

Finite extensions of local fields introduce ramification, residue-degree, and norm behavior in a controlled setting. Comparing these invariants prepares the transition to global field extensions.

10.2.3 · Local norm and solvability questions

Norm equations and polynomial equations can be tested over local fields for necessary conditions on global solutions. The section frames these tests carefully and distinguishes local evidence from a global theorem.

11 · Selected modern directions

The final chapter sketches how classical number theory opens into arithmetic geometry and automorphic ideas. Its purpose is to establish vocabulary and a map for further study, not to replace dedicated graduate courses in these subjects.

11.1 · Elliptic curves and heights

Elliptic curves turn cubic equations into algebraic groups whose rational points carry rich arithmetic structure. Height functions measure the arithmetic complexity of points and support finiteness and growth theorems.

11.1.1 · Rational points

Rational points are solutions whose coordinates lie in the rational numbers and form a group under the elliptic-curve law. Their structure connects Diophantine equations to geometry and arithmetic.

11.1.2 · Torsion

Torsion points are points of finite order in the elliptic-curve group. Their classification illustrates how local and global constraints restrict rational solutions.

11.1.3 · Height motivation

Heights assign a size to rational points in a way compatible with the group law up to controlled error. They provide the quantitative tool behind finiteness arguments and the study of point growth.

11.2 · Modular and arithmetic connections

Modular forms connect analytic transformation laws to arithmetic coefficients and Galois representations. L-functions unify these data and provide a framework for stating major theorems and conjectures.

11.2.1 · Modular forms

A modular form is a highly symmetric analytic function whose Fourier coefficients carry arithmetic information. Its transformation law makes it a bridge between complex analysis, geometry, and number theory.

11.2.2 · L-functions

L-functions attach analytic objects to arithmetic structures such as characters, number fields, or modular forms. Their analytic behavior is expected to encode deep information about the underlying objects.

11.2.3 · Statement and scope of a selected theorem

A selected theorem should be stated with its exact hypotheses, conclusion, and dependency map before any proof is attempted. The chosen example will demonstrate how the elementary and algebraic foundations developed earlier support a modern result.
