Part I · Foundations and Core Language

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

1 · Homotopy and fundamental groups

Homotopy compares maps through continuous deformation. The fundamental group records based loops modulo based homotopy.

1.1 · Homotopies

Homotopy compares maps through continuous deformation. It provides a flexible equivalence notion that preserves many algebraic invariants.

1.1.1 · Homotopy equivalence

A homotopy equivalence is a map with an inverse up to homotopy. It identifies spaces with the same homotopy-level structure.

1.1.2 · Deformation retracts

A deformation retract continuously shrinks a space onto a subspace while fixing that subspace. It gives a particularly concrete homotopy equivalence.

1.1.3 · Homotopy invariance

Homotopy invariance means that homotopic maps induce the same invariant map, with the relevant basepoint conventions. This allows continuous deformations to simplify computations.

1.2 · Fundamental group

The fundamental group records based loops modulo based homotopy. It detects obstructions to contracting loops and supports covering-space theory.

1.2.1 · Loops and basepoints

A based loop begins and ends at a specified point. Concatenation and reversal define the fundamental group operation and inverse.

1.2.2 · Van Kampen theorem

Van Kampen computes a fundamental group from compatible open pieces under its connectivity hypotheses. It describes how generators and relations assemble across overlaps.

1.2.3 · Computations

Circles, bouquets, and surfaces provide standard fundamental-group computations. These examples establish how geometric identifications produce algebraic relations.

2 · Covering spaces

Covering maps locally present a space as disjoint copies of the base. Deck transformations are automorphisms of a covering over its base.

2.1 · Covering maps

Covering maps locally present a space as disjoint copies of the base. Lifting properties translate topology into discrete and group-theoretic data.

2.1.1 · Lifting properties

Lifting asks whether a map into the base can be realized upstairs in the covering space. Existence and uniqueness depend on the map, basepoint, and covering hypotheses.

2.1.2 · Path and homotopy lifting

Paths lift uniquely after choosing a starting point, and suitable homotopies lift compatibly. These facts connect covering spaces to the fundamental group.

2.1.3 · Classification hypotheses

The standard subgroup classification assumes a connected, locally path-connected, semilocally simply connected base. Without these assumptions, familiar conclusions can fail.

2.2 · Deck transformations

Deck transformations are automorphisms of a covering over its base. Universal covers make their relation to fundamental groups especially clear.

2.2.1 · Universal covers

A universal cover is a simply connected covering space when it exists. It supplies a common source for connected coverings in the standard setting.

2.2.2 · Group actions

Appropriate properly discontinuous group actions produce covering quotients. Deck groups record the corresponding symmetries upstairs.

2.2.3 · Examples from surfaces

The plane covering a torus and related surface examples make lifting visible. Their deck transformations connect geometry with familiar groups.

3 · Singular homology

Singular homology turns maps from simplices into algebraic chain complexes. Homology assigns algebraic maps to continuous maps.

3.1 · Chain complexes

Singular homology turns maps from simplices into algebraic chain complexes. Its quotient construction measures cycles that do not bound.

3.1.1 · Singular simplices

A singular simplex is a continuous map from a standard simplex into the space. Formal combinations of these maps form the chain groups.

3.1.2 · Boundary maps

Boundary maps take alternating sums of face restrictions. Their successive composition is zero, making homology well defined.

3.1.3 · Homology groups

A homology group is the kernel of a boundary map modulo the image of the previous one. It records cycles that survive after boundaries are identified with zero.

3.2 · Functoriality and invariance

Homology assigns algebraic maps to continuous maps. Its invariance properties allow spaces to be replaced by simpler homotopy models.

3.2.1 · Induced maps

A continuous map sends each singular simplex to its composition with that map. This defines the induced chain and homology maps.

3.2.2 · Homotopy invariance

A homotopy produces a chain homotopy through a prism construction. Consequently, homotopic maps induce the same homology map.

3.2.3 · Homology of standard spaces

Points, spheres, and other basic spaces calibrate the meaning of homology groups. Their calculations become inputs for more complicated decompositions.

Part II · Central Theory and Techniques

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

4 · Exact sequences and computation

Relative homology measures a space with a chosen subspace treated as negligible. Mayer–Vietoris computes homology from overlapping pieces.

4.1 · Pairs and relative homology

Relative homology measures a space with a chosen subspace treated as negligible. Exact sequences and excision connect it to absolute computations.

4.1.1 · Long exact sequence

The long exact sequence of a pair connects subspace, whole-space, and relative homology. Its connecting map records the boundary left inside the subspace.

4.1.2 · Excision

Excision removes suitably contained parts without changing relative homology. It expresses the local nature of the relative construction.

4.1.3 · Quotient comparison

For suitable pairs, relative homology agrees with reduced homology of the quotient space. The good-pair hypotheses are essential to this comparison.

4.2 · Mayer–Vietoris

Mayer–Vietoris computes homology from overlapping pieces. It is a practical local-to-global consequence of exactness and excision.

4.2.1 · Decomposition

A suitable decomposition expresses the space as pieces with a controlled overlap. Their individual homology groups become the inputs to the sequence.

4.2.2 · Connecting maps

The connecting map measures the obstruction to splitting a global cycle into cycles on the pieces. Its construction explains the sequence's exactness.

4.2.3 · Calculations

Sphere and surface calculations illustrate successive use of Mayer–Vietoris. Explicit maps are needed when knowing group ranks alone is insufficient.

5 · CW complexes and cellular methods

CW complexes build spaces by attaching cells of increasing dimension. Cellular homology replaces the large singular chain complex by one generator per cell.

5.1 · Cell structures

CW complexes build spaces by attaching cells of increasing dimension. Their skeletal organization supports efficient homotopy and homology arguments.

5.1.1 · Attaching maps

An attaching map glues the boundary of a cell to the previous skeleton. It determines how that cell changes the space.

5.1.2 · Skeleta

The n-skeleton contains all cells of dimension at most n. These nested subspaces form the filtration used in cellular methods.

5.1.3 · Cellular approximation

Cellular approximation replaces a map between CW complexes by a homotopic cellular map under the standard theorem. It aligns maps with the skeletal structure.

5.2 · Cellular homology

Cellular homology replaces the large singular chain complex by one generator per cell. Attaching-map degrees determine its differentials.

5.2.1 · Boundary matrices

Boundary matrices record incidence coefficients between adjacent cell dimensions. Their kernels and images yield homology groups.

5.2.2 · Degree maps

Degrees measure how attaching maps cover quotient spheres with orientation. These integers provide the cellular boundary coefficients.

5.2.3 · Computations on complexes

Projective spaces and finite CW complexes illustrate compact computations. The examples require tracking attaching maps rather than just counting cells.

6 · Cohomology and products

Cochains are algebraic functions on chains with a dual differential. Cup products give cohomology more structure than a list of groups.

6.1 · Cochains

Cochains are algebraic functions on chains with a dual differential. Coefficients and universal coefficient theorems connect cohomology to homology.

6.1.1 · Cochain complexes

A cochain complex reverses the direction of the chain differential through Hom. Its cohomology consists of cocycles modulo coboundaries.

6.1.2 · Universal coefficient theorem

Universal coefficient theorems relate cohomology with specified coefficients to homology via Hom and Ext terms. They explain the effect of torsion.

6.1.3 · Coefficients

Choosing integer, field, or other coefficients changes what information an invariant retains. Coefficient choices can simplify computation while losing some distinctions.

6.2 · Cup products

Cup products give cohomology more structure than a list of groups. The resulting ring can distinguish spaces with identical additive invariants.

6.2.1 · Graded rings

Cohomology with a suitable coefficient ring is a graded ring under cup product. Graded commutativity introduces signs depending on degree.

6.2.2 · Examples

Tori and projective spaces illustrate different cup-product patterns. These examples show why additive groups alone do not determine cohomology rings.

6.2.3 · Cohomological invariants

Products and operations in cohomology provide obstructions to maps and decompositions. Their naturality transports such information between spaces.

7 · Higher homotopy and characteristic classes

Higher homotopy groups study maps from spheres in dimensions above one. Vector bundles carry linear spaces varying over a topological base.

7.1 · Homotopy groups

Higher homotopy groups study maps from spheres in dimensions above one. Comparison theorems connect them with homology and equivalences.

7.1.1 · Hurewicz theorem

Hurewicz identifies the first relevant homotopy group with homology under connectivity assumptions. It provides a route from geometric maps to computable algebra.

7.1.2 · Whitehead theorem

Whitehead's theorem makes a weak homotopy equivalence between CW complexes a homotopy equivalence. The CW setting is part of the theorem's scope.

7.1.3 · Selected computations

Basic sphere and product computations illustrate both the power and difficulty of higher homotopy. The chosen examples should state imported results explicitly.

7.2 · Bundles and classes

Vector bundles carry linear spaces varying over a topological base. Characteristic classes encode obstructions and invariants of these families.

7.2.1 · Vector bundles

A vector bundle is locally a product with a vector space and has linear transition maps. It generalizes the tangent bundle construction.

7.2.2 · Euler/Chern classes

Euler and Chern classes assign cohomology classes to suitably oriented real or complex bundles. They detect properties such as obstructions to sections or trivializations.

7.2.3 · Classifying spaces

Classifying spaces parametrize bundles through homotopy classes of maps under suitable base-space assumptions. Universal bundles turn bundle invariants into cohomological constructions.

8 · Duality and manifolds

Orientations permit a manifold to carry a fundamental homology class. Poincaré duality relates cohomology and complementary-dimensional homology of manifolds.

8.1 · Orientations and cap product

Orientations permit a manifold to carry a fundamental homology class. Cap products translate this class into duality between homology and cohomology.

8.1.1 · Fundamental classes

A fundamental class encodes a consistent local orientation of a closed oriented manifold. Noncompact or boundary cases require modified formulations.

8.1.2 · Cap product

The cap product combines a cohomology class with a homology class to lower dimension. Capping with the fundamental class is the map in Poincaré duality.

8.1.3 · Intersection pairings

Intersection pairings measure how complementary-dimensional cycles meet. They provide a geometric interpretation of duality and cup products.

8.2 · Poincaré duality

Poincaré duality relates cohomology and complementary-dimensional homology of manifolds. Orientation, compactness, and boundary conditions determine its exact form.

8.2.1 · Statement and proof structure

For a closed oriented n-manifold, capping with the fundamental class gives the standard duality isomorphism. A proof assembles local orientation information through suitable gluing arguments.

8.2.2 · Applications

Duality constrains Betti numbers, intersection forms, and possible manifold topology. It supplies checks on independent homology computations.

8.2.3 · Manifolds with boundary

Manifolds with boundary require relative groups in Poincaré–Lefschetz duality. This keeps track of classes that meet the boundary.

Part III · Further Theory and Applications

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

9 · Spectral sequences

Spectral sequences organize a filtered computation into successive approximations. Guided examples turn the spectral-sequence machinery into an actual calculation.

9.1 · Filtered complexes

Spectral sequences organize a filtered computation into successive approximations. Their usefulness depends on both differentials and convergence.

9.1.1 · Filtrations

A filtration is a nested sequence of subobjects compatible with the differential. It separates a complex into graded layers.

9.1.2 · Pages and differentials

Each page consists of groups and a differential whose homology forms the next page. Surviving classes represent progressively more accurate information.

9.1.3 · Convergence conditions

Convergence hypotheses ensure that stabilized pages describe an associated graded target. Without them, formal page calculations may not identify the desired invariant.

9.2 · Guided computations

Guided examples turn the spectral-sequence machinery into an actual calculation. Edge maps and extension problems explain what the pages do and do not determine.

9.2.1 · Serre or cellular examples

Serre spectral sequences use suitable fibrations to relate base, fiber, and total-space invariants. Cellular filtrations provide another accessible source of examples.

9.2.2 · Edge maps

Edge maps connect limiting information to recognizable maps on low-filtration pieces. They help interpret abstract computations geometrically.

9.2.3 · Extension problems

An associated graded group may not determine the original group uniquely. Extension problems resolve how the surviving pieces fit together.

10 · Generalized theories and capstone

Axioms characterize homology theories through their behavior under basic constructions. The capstone joins a chosen computation with an audit of its logical dependencies.

10.1 · Homology axioms

Axioms characterize homology theories through their behavior under basic constructions. They distinguish ordinary theory from broader generalized theories.

10.1.1 · Eilenberg–Steenrod framework

The Eilenberg–Steenrod axioms formalize homotopy, exactness, excision, and related properties. The dimension axiom singles out ordinary homology in the classical framework.

10.1.2 · Uniqueness

Under the standard axioms and coefficient specification, ordinary homology is determined on suitable categories of spaces. The category and axioms must be stated with the uniqueness claim.

10.1.3 · Representability motivation

Representability realizes generalized cohomology through homotopy classes of maps to suitable spaces or spectra. It links algebraic invariants back to homotopy geometry.

10.2 · Selected synthesis

The capstone joins a chosen computation with an audit of its logical dependencies. Advanced theories are introduced as further routes rather than completed developments.

10.2.1 · K-theory or stable homotopy overview

K-theory studies bundles and their stable algebra, while stable homotopy studies maps after suspension stabilization. Both extend the range of ordinary homological invariants.

10.2.2 · Proof dependency audit

A dependency audit records the exact theorem and hypothesis behind each imported step. It distinguishes computed conclusions from assumed comparison results.

10.2.3 · Project case study

A selected space or bundle supplies a complete example from definitions to invariants. Its final record should include the calculation, its limits, and a five-component workflow.
