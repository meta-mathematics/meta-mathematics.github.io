Part I · Foundations and Core Language

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

1 · Euclidean spaces and linear approximation

Norms measure lengths and errors in vector spaces. Differentiability approximates a nonlinear map by a linear map at a point.

1.1 · Normed spaces

Norms measure lengths and errors in vector spaces. Finite-dimensional equivalence makes many analytic properties independent of the chosen norm.

1.1.1 · Norms and equivalent norms

Equivalent norms bound one another by fixed positive constants. They therefore give the same convergence and continuity notions.

1.1.2 · Convergence

Convergence means that distance to a proposed limit tends to zero. It supplies the language for continuity and differentiation.

1.1.3 · Compactness in finite dimensions

In Euclidean space, compact sets are precisely the closed bounded sets. This equivalence supplies convergent subsequences and attained extrema.

1.2 · Differentiability

Differentiability approximates a nonlinear map by a linear map at a point. The section distinguishes this approximation from merely having partial derivatives.

1.2.1 · Fréchet derivative

The Fréchet derivative gives a linear approximation with error smaller than the input increment. It unifies differentiation in normed spaces.

1.2.2 · Coordinate derivatives

Partial derivatives measure variation along coordinate directions. Their existence alone does not guarantee total differentiability.

1.2.3 · Chain rule

The chain rule differentiates compositions by composing their derivatives. It makes coordinate changes and nested models manageable.

2 · Local structure of mappings

An invertible derivative can imply that a nonlinear map has a local inverse. The implicit function theorem solves some variables in terms of the others.

2.1 · Inverse function theorem

An invertible derivative can imply that a nonlinear map has a local inverse. The theorem turns a linear algebra condition into a local geometric conclusion.

2.1.1 · Statement and hypotheses

The standard theorem assumes a continuously differentiable map with invertible derivative at the chosen point. These hypotheses define the neighborhood where an inverse can be constructed.

2.1.2 · Local inverse construction

A local inverse may be constructed through a contraction argument near the point. The construction explains both uniqueness and differentiability of the inverse.

2.1.3 · Quantitative estimates

Quantitative estimates bound neighborhood size and inverse sensitivity. They clarify what happens when the derivative approaches singularity.

2.2 · Implicit function theorem

The implicit function theorem solves some variables in terms of the others. It explains when equations define smooth graphs and constraint surfaces.

2.2.1 · Regular level sets

A regular level set has derivative of full target rank along the set. This condition gives the expected local dimension.

2.2.2 · Solving constrained equations

An invertible partial derivative allows selected unknowns to be solved locally. This reduces a constrained system to a graph.

2.2.3 · Parameter dependence

Solutions may vary smoothly with external parameters under uniform nonsingularity assumptions. This gives local sensitivity information for implicit models.

3 · Higher-order calculus

Higher derivatives are multilinear maps recording successive variations. Taylor's theorem separates a local polynomial approximation from its remainder.

3.1 · Multilinear derivatives

Higher derivatives are multilinear maps recording successive variations. Their algebra controls Taylor expansions and curvature tests.

3.1.1 · Bilinear and higher derivatives

Second derivatives are bilinear, while higher derivatives accept several direction vectors. Their evaluations measure mixed directional changes.

3.1.2 · Symmetry

Sufficient continuity of higher derivatives gives symmetry under permutation of directions. This justifies exchanging mixed derivatives.

3.1.3 · Operator norms

An operator norm bounds a multilinear map on products of unit balls. It converts derivative information into uniform error estimates.

3.2 · Taylor's theorem

Taylor's theorem separates a local polynomial approximation from its remainder. Precise remainder bounds determine when the approximation is reliable.

3.2.1 · Multivariable expansion

The multivariable Taylor polynomial combines derivative tensors with powers of the increment. It describes local behavior in every direction.

3.2.2 · Integral remainder

An integral remainder averages the next derivative along a connecting segment. It provides an exact identity under suitable regularity.

3.2.3 · Error bounds

Derivative bounds control the size of the omitted terms. Such estimates support approximation, optimization, and stability arguments.

Part II · Central Theory and Techniques

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

4 · Integration in several variables

Several-variable integration aggregates functions over regions in Euclidean space. A coordinate transformation changes both the integrand and the volume element.

4.1 · Riemann and Lebesgue integration

Several-variable integration aggregates functions over regions in Euclidean space. Comparing Riemann and Lebesgue constructions clarifies which limit operations are allowed.

4.1.1 · Measurability context

Measurability specifies which functions and sets admit measure-theoretic integration. It is a prerequisite for the general interchange theorems.

4.1.2 · Iterated integrals

Iterated integration integrates one variable at a time. Its relation to a joint integral depends on the integrand and measure spaces.

4.1.3 · Fubini and Tonelli

Tonelli treats nonnegative functions, while Fubini treats integrable functions under standard product-measure hypotheses. These results justify exchanging integration order.

4.2 · Change of variables

A coordinate transformation changes both the integrand and the volume element. The change-of-variables theorem expresses that adjustment through the derivative.

4.2.1 · Jacobian determinant

The Jacobian determinant measures signed local volume scaling. Its absolute value appears in scalar volume integrals.

4.2.2 · Orientation

Orientation distinguishes positively and negatively aligned coordinate systems. It determines signs in integration of differential forms.

4.2.3 · Geometric volume scaling

A differentiable map resembles its derivative on small regions. Linear volume scaling therefore motivates the Jacobian factor in integration.

5 · Vector calculus

Line and surface integrals measure accumulated quantities along geometric objects. Classical vector-calculus theorems relate interior derivatives to boundary integrals.

5.1 · Line and surface integrals

Line and surface integrals measure accumulated quantities along geometric objects. Their definitions must account for parametrization and orientation.

5.1.1 · Parametrized curves

A parametrized curve describes a path by a differentiable map from an interval. It converts geometric integration into one-variable integration.

5.1.2 · Flux

Flux integrates the normal component of a vector field across a surface. It measures net passage through that oriented surface.

5.1.3 · Orientation conventions

Orientation fixes the direction of traversal or the choice of normal. Consistent conventions are essential when matching boundary and interior integrals.

5.2 · Classical integral identities

Classical vector-calculus theorems relate interior derivatives to boundary integrals. The section emphasizes their regularity assumptions and orientation rules.

5.2.1 · Green's theorem

Green's theorem relates a planar line integral to an area integral of derivatives. It is the two-dimensional model for more general boundary identities.

5.2.2 · Divergence theorem

The divergence theorem relates outward flux to the volume integral of divergence. It provides a mathematical form of balance over a region.

5.2.3 · Stokes' theorem in space

Stokes' theorem in space relates circulation around a boundary to surface curl. The boundary orientation must agree with the surface normal.

6 · Differential forms

Exterior algebra packages alternating multilinear quantities. The exterior derivative extends gradient, curl, and divergence in one framework.

6.1 · Exterior algebra

Exterior algebra packages alternating multilinear quantities. It supplies a coordinate-independent language for oriented area and volume.

6.1.1 · Alternating tensors

Alternating tensors change sign when two inputs are interchanged. They vanish on repeated inputs and encode oriented geometry.

6.1.2 · Wedge product

The wedge product combines alternating forms into higher-degree forms. Its graded sign rule records the ordering of directions.

6.1.3 · Pullback

Pullback transports forms from a target space to a source through the derivative. It makes integration compatible with parametrizations.

6.2 · Exterior derivative

The exterior derivative extends gradient, curl, and divergence in one framework. Its algebraic identities reveal which differential expressions can have potentials.

6.2.1 · Coordinate formula

The coordinate formula differentiates coefficients and wedges them with coordinate differentials. It turns an intrinsic operation into a practical computation.

6.2.2 · Naturality

Exterior differentiation commutes with pullback by smooth maps. This naturality ensures that calculations agree across coordinate systems.

6.2.3 · Closed and exact forms

Closed forms have zero exterior derivative, while exact forms are derivatives of other forms. Their difference measures local and global obstructions to potentials.

7 · Generalized Stokes theorem

Integration on manifolds assembles Euclidean integrals through coordinate charts. Generalized Stokes equates the integral of an exterior derivative with a boundary integral.

7.1 · Integration on manifolds

Integration on manifolds assembles Euclidean integrals through coordinate charts. Orientation and localization make the assembled value consistent.

7.1.1 · Orientations

An orientation is a compatible choice of orientation in all coordinate charts. It determines the sign of integrals of top-degree forms.

7.1.2 · Boundary

The boundary of a smooth manifold inherits an orientation from the interior. This induced orientation fixes the sign in Stokes' theorem.

7.1.3 · Partitions of unity motivation

Partitions of unity split a global integrand into chart-supported pieces. They allow local integration formulas to be combined consistently.

7.2 · Stokes and consequences

Generalized Stokes equates the integral of an exterior derivative with a boundary integral. Its special cases unify the fundamental integral identities of calculus.

7.2.1 · Classical theorems as special cases

Green, divergence, and classical Stokes formulas follow from appropriate choices of forms and domains. Deriving them reveals their shared structure.

7.2.2 · Fundamental theorem of calculus

On an interval, Stokes' theorem becomes endpoint evaluation of an antiderivative. This recovers the fundamental theorem of calculus.

7.2.3 · Degree motivation

Degree counts oriented preimages of a regular value under suitable hypotheses. Integration provides a bridge from local derivatives to this global invariant.

Part III · Further Theory and Applications

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

8 · Optimization and geometry

Constrained optimization studies extrema on sets defined by equations. Implicit geometry interprets equations as local geometric objects.

8.1 · Constrained extrema

Constrained optimization studies extrema on sets defined by equations. Regularity allows derivatives of the objective to be compared with constraint normals.

8.1.1 · Lagrange multipliers

Lagrange multipliers express stationarity as a linear combination of constraint gradients. Their necessity requires an appropriate constraint qualification.

8.1.2 · Regular constraints

Independent constraint gradients define a smooth feasible surface locally. Failure of independence can invalidate the usual multiplier equations.

8.1.3 · Second-order tests

Second-order tests examine the Hessian of the Lagrangian on feasible tangent directions. Positivity or negativity there can certify a strict constrained extremum.

8.2 · Implicit geometry

Implicit geometry interprets equations as local geometric objects. Derivatives identify tangent spaces and coordinate descriptions of those objects.

8.2.1 · Tangent spaces to level sets

At a regular point, tangent vectors to a level set lie in the derivative's kernel. This identifies allowable first-order motions under the constraint.

8.2.2 · Regular values

A regular value has full-rank derivative at every point in its preimage. The regular-value theorem then gives a smooth level manifold.

8.2.3 · Local parametrizations

Local parametrizations express a manifold using independent coordinates. The implicit function theorem supplies these parametrizations near regular points.

9 · Parameter-dependent problems

Differentiation under an integral exchanges a derivative with an averaging operation. The final section combines local solvability with coordinate and approximation methods.

9.1 · Differentiation under the integral

Differentiation under an integral exchanges a derivative with an averaging operation. The exchange requires control of parameter-dependent difference quotients or derivatives.

9.1.1 · Dominating functions

An integrable dominating function bounds the relevant parameter derivatives near a point. Dominated convergence can then justify moving the derivative inside.

9.1.2 · Uniformity hypotheses

Uniform hypotheses keep estimates valid throughout a parameter neighborhood. They support continuity and repeated differentiation of the resulting integral.

9.1.3 · Examples

Parameterized exponential and kernel integrals illustrate valid interchanges. Contrasting examples show why pointwise differentiability alone is insufficient.

9.2 · Applications and synthesis

The final section combines local solvability with coordinate and approximation methods. Its examples should expose all regularity and quantitative assumptions.

9.2.1 · Inverse function applications

The inverse function theorem resolves local uniqueness and invertibility questions. Applications require checking the derivative at the actual point of interest.

9.2.2 · Local coordinates

Local coordinates turn geometric constraints into ordinary Euclidean variables. Their transition maps preserve the differentiable structure of the calculation.

9.2.3 · Rigorous approximation estimates

Approximation estimates track remainders with explicit norms and constants. They connect formal expansions to statements with controlled error.
