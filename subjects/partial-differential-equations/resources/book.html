Part I · Foundations and Core Language

1 · First-order equations and classical solutions

Equation classification begins with order, linearity, and the role of principal derivatives. Transport and conservation equations describe motion and balance.

1.1 · Equation types

Equation classification begins with order, linearity, and the role of principal derivatives. These distinctions determine which local solution methods are plausible.

1.1.1 · Order and linearity

The order is the highest derivative order, while linearity describes how the unknown and its derivatives enter. These properties guide the first division of PDE methods.

1.1.2 · Characteristic curves

Characteristic curves turn suitable first-order PDEs into ordinary differential equations along paths. They reveal how data propagate and where smooth solutions can break down.

1.1.3 · Canonical forms

Canonical forms simplify an equation through adapted coordinates or transformations. Their availability depends on the equation's coefficients and local type.

1.2 · Transport and conservation

Transport and conservation equations describe motion and balance. They motivate both smooth evolution and weak solutions after singularities form.

1.2.1 · Cauchy problems

A Cauchy problem prescribes initial data on an appropriate hypersurface. Noncharacteristic and regularity assumptions determine local solvability.

1.2.2 · Shocks

Shocks are discontinuities that can form even from smooth data in nonlinear conservation laws. They force a transition from classical to weak solution concepts.

1.2.3 · Entropy conditions

Entropy conditions select physically and mathematically admissible weak solutions in relevant conservation-law settings. They rule out some solutions that satisfy the weak equation alone.

2 · Second-order linear equations

Second-order classification uses the principal part rather than lower-order terms. Canonical equations provide benchmark models for the main PDE types.

2.1 · Classification

Second-order classification uses the principal part rather than lower-order terms. Elliptic, parabolic, and hyperbolic behavior lead to different data requirements.

2.1.1 · Elliptic, parabolic, and hyperbolic forms

Elliptic, parabolic, and hyperbolic types describe different signatures or structures of principal derivatives. The familiar scalar classification must be adapted carefully for systems.

2.1.2 · Coordinate changes

Coordinate changes can simplify the principal part locally. They also transform coefficients and data, which must be tracked in the new formulation.

2.2 · Canonical examples

Canonical equations provide benchmark models for the main PDE types. Their explicit solutions motivate broader theories.

2.2.1 · Laplace, heat, and wave equations

Laplace, heat, and wave equations model equilibrium, diffusion, and propagation respectively. Comparing them reveals why one universal solution method is insufficient.

2.2.2 · Initial and boundary data

Initial and boundary data specify how a PDE is posed on its domain. Their compatibility and type influence existence, uniqueness, and regularity.

3 · Distributions and weak derivatives

Distribution theory interprets differentiation through action on smooth test functions. Weak formulations shift derivatives away from the unknown by testing and integration.

3.1 · Test functions

Distribution theory interprets differentiation through action on smooth test functions. This makes equations meaningful for nonsmooth objects.

3.1.1 · Support and topology

Test functions have compact support and a topology controlling derivatives on fixed compact sets. These conditions support integration by parts without boundary terms.

3.1.2 · Distributions

A distribution is a continuous linear functional on test functions. Locally integrable functions and point masses are standard examples.

3.1.3 · Derivative operations

Distributional differentiation moves derivatives onto the test function with the appropriate sign. The operation is defined even when pointwise derivatives do not exist.

3.2 · Weak formulations

Weak formulations shift derivatives away from the unknown by testing and integration. They specify exactly what it means to satisfy an equation with limited regularity.

3.2.1 · Integration by parts

Integration by parts transfers derivatives and exposes boundary terms. It determines the spaces and data entering a weak formulation.

3.2.2 · Weak solutions

A weak solution satisfies the tested equation in the specified function space. The term is incomplete without its test class and boundary interpretation.

3.2.3 · Uniqueness frameworks

Uniqueness may follow from energy, comparison, contraction, or monotonicity arguments. The solution class is part of every uniqueness statement.

Part II · Central Theory and Techniques

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

4 · Sobolev spaces

Sobolev spaces measure integrability of functions together with weak derivatives. Embedding and trace theorems turn weak derivative control into other regularity information.

4.1 · Definitions and norms

Sobolev spaces measure integrability of functions together with weak derivatives. Their norms make weak PDE analysis quantitative.

4.1.1 · Weak derivatives

A weak derivative is an integrable function representing the corresponding distributional derivative. It extends differentiation while retaining function-space estimates.

4.1.2 · Density

Density theorems approximate Sobolev functions by smoother ones under suitable domain conditions. They allow identities proved for smooth functions to extend by limits.

4.1.3 · Extension operators

An extension operator continues functions beyond a domain while controlling a Sobolev norm. Its existence depends on domain regularity and the chosen space.

4.2 · Embedding and trace theorems

Embedding and trace theorems turn weak derivative control into other regularity information. Compact versions provide convergent subsequences for existence arguments.

4.2.1 · Sobolev inequalities

Sobolev inequalities bound a function in one norm by derivatives in another. The dimension and exponents determine the admissible estimate.

4.2.2 · Compact embeddings

Compact embeddings improve boundedness into precompactness under suitable domain and exponent conditions. They are central to passing through nonlinear terms in limits.

4.2.3 · Boundary values

Trace operators define boundary values for Sobolev functions when pointwise restriction is unavailable. The resulting boundary space depends on the interior regularity.

5 · Elliptic equations: existence

Variational formulations seek solutions through bilinear forms on energy spaces. A priori estimates bound solutions using only the given data and coefficients.

5.1 · Variational formulation

Variational formulations seek solutions through bilinear forms on energy spaces. Coercivity provides the estimate that supports existence and uniqueness.

5.1.1 · Energy spaces

An energy space is chosen so the weak equation and its natural estimates are well defined. Boundary conditions may be encoded by a closed subspace.

5.1.2 · Coercivity

Coercivity bounds a bilinear form below by a positive multiple of the squared norm in the standard Hilbert setting. It prevents uncontrolled directions in the variational problem.

5.1.3 · Lax–Milgram theorem

Lax–Milgram solves a bounded coercive bilinear-form equation against a continuous linear functional. It is a basic existence theorem for weak elliptic boundary problems.

5.2 · A priori estimates

A priori estimates bound solutions using only the given data and coefficients. They support uniqueness, stability, and approximation limits.

5.2.1 · Energy estimates

Energy estimates test an equation with a suitable function related to its solution. The resulting inequality controls natural solution norms.

5.2.2 · Uniqueness

Uniqueness follows when the difference of two solutions has zero norm under an appropriate estimate. The same boundary and solution-space assumptions must apply to both.

5.2.3 · Dependence on data

Continuous dependence bounds changes in the solution by changes in data. Together with existence and uniqueness, it contributes to well-posedness.

6 · Elliptic equations: regularity

Elliptic regularity asks when weak solutions have additional derivatives or continuity. Boundary regularity depends on the equation, boundary conditions, and geometry of the domain.

6.1 · Interior regularity

Elliptic regularity asks when weak solutions have additional derivatives or continuity. Interior results separate coefficient effects from boundary geometry.

6.1.1 · Difference quotients

Difference quotients approximate derivatives while remaining legitimate weak test constructions. Uniform estimates then establish additional Sobolev regularity.

6.1.2 · Elliptic estimates

Elliptic estimates bound higher regularity by the equation's forcing and lower solution norms. Their local and global versions have different hypotheses.

6.1.3 · Smooth coefficients

Smooth coefficients permit repeated regularity arguments when the forcing is sufficiently smooth. Bootstrapping explains why many weak elliptic solutions become classical in the interior.

6.2 · Boundary regularity

Boundary regularity depends on the equation, boundary conditions, and geometry of the domain. Interior smoothness alone does not guarantee smoothness up to the boundary.

6.2.1 · Boundary conditions

Dirichlet, Neumann, and related conditions constrain different boundary quantities. Their compatibility and regularity affect the available solution estimates.

6.2.2 · Trace spaces

Trace spaces describe admissible boundary data for Sobolev solutions. Matching the correct trace space prevents ill-defined boundary prescriptions.

6.2.3 · Regularity limitations

Corners, rough coefficients, or irregular data can block higher regularity. Explicit limitations clarify why regularity conclusions need detailed assumptions.

7 · Maximum principles and eigenvalues

Maximum principles compare solutions through sign and extremum information. Elliptic eigenvalue problems combine PDE estimates with spectral theory.

7.1 · Weak and strong principles

Maximum principles compare solutions through sign and extremum information. Their precise form depends on coefficients, boundary conditions, and the solution class.

7.1.1 · Comparison

Comparison orders a subsolution and supersolution using their boundary data. It often gives uniqueness without constructing an explicit solution.

7.1.2 · Hopf lemma

Hopf's boundary lemma gives a strict boundary derivative conclusion at an appropriate extremum. Domain geometry and nondegeneracy assumptions are essential.

7.1.3 · Positivity

Positivity arguments show when nonnegative data yield positive interior solutions. Strong maximum principles rule out nontrivial interior zero minima in appropriate settings.

7.2 · Spectral consequences

Elliptic eigenvalue problems combine PDE estimates with spectral theory. Positivity and variational methods reveal special features of the lowest eigenvalues.

7.2.1 · Eigenvalue problems

An eigenvalue problem seeks nonzero solutions whose operator image is a scalar multiple of themselves. Boundary conditions and function spaces determine the spectrum.

7.2.2 · Rayleigh quotient

The Rayleigh quotient compares an energy with a squared norm. Minimization often characterizes the first eigenvalue for self-adjoint elliptic problems.

7.2.3 · Maximum principle applications

Maximum principles constrain signs of eigenfunctions and can identify principal eigenvalues. Applications must match the operator and boundary hypotheses of the chosen principle.

8 · Parabolic equations

The heat equation models diffusion and introduces smoothing in time. Abstract evolution frameworks treat time-dependent PDEs as operator equations.

8.1 · Heat equation methods

The heat equation models diffusion and introduces smoothing in time. Explicit kernels and energy estimates provide complementary methods.

8.1.1 · Fundamental solution

The heat fundamental solution represents evolution of concentrated initial data. Convolution constructs solutions on Euclidean space under appropriate assumptions.

8.1.2 · Energy estimates

Parabolic energy estimates balance norm decay against spatial dissipation. They control solutions over time using initial and forcing data.

8.1.3 · Smoothing

Heat evolution gains spatial regularity for positive times in standard settings. Estimates quantify how this regularity depends on elapsed time.

8.2 · Evolution frameworks

Abstract evolution frameworks treat time-dependent PDEs as operator equations. They organize existence and qualitative estimates beyond explicit kernels.

8.2.1 · Semigroups

A semigroup represents consistent evolution over successive time intervals. Its generator encodes the differential operator and its domain.

8.2.2 · Maximum principle

Parabolic maximum principles bound later values by initial and boundary information under suitable conditions. They provide comparison and uniqueness tools.

8.2.3 · Weak existence

Weak existence can be obtained through approximation, uniform estimates, and compactness. The final limit must satisfy the equation, initial condition, and boundary conditions in specified senses.

9 · Hyperbolic equations

The wave equation describes finite-speed propagation and conservative behavior. Hyperbolic systems generalize scalar wave propagation with multiple characteristic modes.

9.1 · Wave equation

The wave equation describes finite-speed propagation and conservative behavior. Energy estimates provide the central mechanism for well-posedness.

9.1.1 · Energy conservation

For an unforced wave equation with appropriate boundary conditions, total kinetic-plus-potential energy is conserved. This controls the evolution in an energy space.

9.1.2 · Finite speed

Finite propagation speed confines influence to characteristic cones. It distinguishes wave behavior from the instantaneous spreading of the heat equation.

9.1.3 · Well-posedness

Well-posedness requires existence, uniqueness, and continuous dependence in a specified class. Energy methods establish these properties in standard linear wave settings.

9.2 · Systems and propagation

Hyperbolic systems generalize scalar wave propagation with multiple characteristic modes. Boundaries and dispersion introduce additional analytic issues.

9.2.1 · Characteristics

Characteristics determine propagation directions from the principal symbol. Their structure can vary across systems and coefficient regimes.

9.2.2 · Boundary conditions

Hyperbolic boundary data must match incoming characteristic information. Incorrectly prescribing all components can overdetermine a problem.

9.2.3 · Dispersive previews

Dispersive estimates measure spreading through oscillatory cancellation rather than diffusion. They prepare the study of nonlinear wave and related equations.

10 · Variational and nonlinear PDE

Variational methods obtain PDEs from extrema of functionals. Nonlinear existence arguments use fixed points, monotonicity, or variational structure.

10.1 · Calculus of variations

Variational methods obtain PDEs from extrema of functionals. Existence depends on compactness and lower semicontinuity rather than a formal derivative alone.

10.1.1 · Direct method

The direct method extracts a limit from a minimizing sequence using coercivity and compactness. Lower semicontinuity then identifies that limit as a minimizer.

10.1.2 · Euler–Lagrange equations

Euler–Lagrange equations express vanishing of the first variation. Their derivation requires admissible variations and appropriate differentiability.

10.1.3 · Constraints

Constraints restrict allowable variations or introduce multipliers. They change both the existence problem and the resulting stationarity equations.

10.2 · Nonlinear methods

Nonlinear existence arguments use fixed points, monotonicity, or variational structure. Each method requires estimates tailored to the equation.

10.2.1 · Fixed points

Fixed-point theorems turn a nonlinear equation into a self-map problem. Contraction, continuity, and compactness assumptions distinguish different theorems.

10.2.2 · Monotonicity

Monotone operators compare differences of outputs with differences of inputs through a dual pairing. Coercivity and continuity variants lead to nonlinear existence results.

10.2.3 · Nonlinear elliptic examples

Semilinear and quasilinear elliptic equations illustrate different nonlinear mechanisms. Selected examples should expose both the existence argument and any separate uniqueness conditions.

11 · Advanced topics and synthesis

Conservation laws and fluid equations combine nonlinear transport with weak solution issues. Geometric and dispersive PDE connect analytic estimates with evolving geometry or oscillatory propagation.

11.1 · Conservation laws and fluids

Conservation laws and fluid equations combine nonlinear transport with weak solution issues. Their study demands careful distinctions between existence, uniqueness, and regularity.

11.1.1 · Weak entropy solutions

Weak entropy solutions satisfy both a distributional conservation law and admissibility inequalities. These extra conditions help select meaningful evolutions.

11.1.2 · Shocks

Shock waves satisfy jump relations derived from conservation. Their formation and interaction illustrate the limits of classical solutions.

11.1.3 · Navier–Stokes overview

The Navier–Stokes equations combine momentum balance, viscosity, and incompressibility. Standard existence results and unresolved regularity questions must be distinguished by dimension and solution class.

11.2 · Geometric and dispersive PDE

Geometric and dispersive PDE connect analytic estimates with evolving geometry or oscillatory propagation. The final section organizes a selected route through its proof dependencies.

11.2.1 · Selected research frontiers

A chosen advanced topic should begin with a precise equation and a dated statement of the target result. Its open and established components must be kept explicit.

11.2.2 · Imported estimates

Imported estimates must state their domains, regularity, constants, and applicable solution classes. Recording these details prevents unjustified transfer between equations.

11.2.3 · Proof-gate analysis

A proof gate is a stated obligation that must be met before a later inference is accepted. Mapping these gates exposes missing hypotheses and unresolved steps.
