Part I · Foundations and Core Language

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

1 · Measure spaces and construction

Measure theory assigns sizes to sets within a specified measurable structure. Measurable functions connect measurable spaces and admit systematic approximation.

1.1 · Sigma-algebras and measures

Measure theory assigns sizes to sets within a specified measurable structure. Construction theorems extend elementary size assignments to useful sigma-algebras.

1.1.1 · Measurable sets

Measurable sets belong to the sigma-algebra on which a measure is defined. Closure under countable operations makes limit arguments possible.

1.1.2 · Measures and outer measure

Measures are countably additive on measurable sets, while outer measures are defined on all subsets. Outer measure is a common starting point for measure construction.

1.1.3 · Extension theorem

Extension theorems extend a premeasure from an algebra to a generated sigma-algebra. Sigma-finiteness gives a standard uniqueness hypothesis.

1.2 · Measurable functions

Measurable functions connect measurable spaces and admit systematic approximation. Identifying functions that agree almost everywhere prepares the construction of function spaces.

1.2.1 · Simple functions

Simple functions take finitely many values on measurable sets. They serve as elementary building blocks for integration.

1.2.2 · Limits of measurable functions

Pointwise limits of measurable functions remain measurable where defined. This stability allows approximation procedures to stay inside the theory.

1.2.3 · Almost-everywhere equivalence

Functions agreeing outside a null set have the same integral when integrable. Function spaces identify such functions to obtain genuine norms.

2 · Lebesgue integration

Lebesgue integration begins with nonnegative simple functions and passes to limits. Convergence theorems specify when limiting functions and integrals can be compared.

2.1 · Integral construction

Lebesgue integration begins with nonnegative simple functions and passes to limits. Signed or complex integration then requires control of absolute size.

2.1.1 · Nonnegative functions

The integral of a nonnegative measurable function is defined through simple-function approximation. It may be infinite without being undefined.

2.1.2 · Simple approximation

Nonnegative measurable functions admit increasing simple approximations. This construction links a general integral to explicitly computable pieces.

2.1.3 · Integrable functions

An integrable function has finite integral of its absolute value. This condition prevents ambiguous cancellation between infinite positive and negative contributions.

2.2 · Convergence theorems

Convergence theorems specify when limiting functions and integrals can be compared. Their hypotheses are central to rigorous approximation.

2.2.1 · Monotone convergence

Monotone convergence exchanges increasing nonnegative limits with integration. It also permits the limiting integral to be infinite.

2.2.2 · Fatou lemma

Fatou's lemma bounds the integral of a lower limit by the lower limit of integrals for nonnegative functions. It provides an inequality when full interchange is unavailable.

2.2.3 · Dominated convergence

Dominated convergence allows almost-everywhere limits under a common integrable bound. It is a primary tool for parameterized integrals and approximation.

3 · Product measures and differentiation

Product measures support integration over several variables. Differentiation of measures describes one measure through another or through local averages.

3.1 · Product integration

Product measures support integration over several variables. Tonelli and Fubini give distinct conditions for iterated integration.

3.1.1 · Product sigma-algebras

The product sigma-algebra is generated by measurable rectangles. It supplies the measurable structure for joint variables.

3.1.2 · Tonelli theorem

Tonelli's theorem interchanges integrals of nonnegative measurable functions under standard product hypotheses. Infinite values are permitted.

3.1.3 · Fubini theorem

Fubini's theorem applies to absolutely integrable functions on product spaces. It equates the joint integral with almost-everywhere defined iterated integrals.

3.2 · Differentiation of measures

Differentiation of measures describes one measure through another or through local averages. The resulting densities connect abstract integration to pointwise behavior.

3.2.1 · Absolute continuity

A measure is absolutely continuous with respect to another when it vanishes on that measure's null sets. This is the key qualitative condition for density representation.

3.2.2 · Radon–Nikodym theorem

The Radon–Nikodym theorem represents an absolutely continuous measure by an integrable density under appropriate finiteness assumptions. The density is unique almost everywhere.

3.2.3 · Lebesgue differentiation

Lebesgue differentiation recovers a locally integrable function from shrinking local averages almost everywhere. It connects integral information to pointwise values.

Part II · Central Theory and Techniques

This part groups chapters in this subject’s graduate book.

4 · Lp spaces

Lp spaces measure integrability through powers or essential bounds. Duality represents continuous linear functionals through integration.

4.1 · Inequalities and structure

Lp spaces measure integrability through powers or essential bounds. Their inequalities and completeness make them reliable spaces for limits and operators.

4.1.1 · Hölder inequality

Hölder's inequality bounds a product integral by conjugate Lp norms. It identifies natural pairings between functions.

4.1.2 · Minkowski inequality

Minkowski's inequality gives the triangle inequality for Lp norms when p is at least one. It makes these spaces normed linear spaces.

4.1.3 · Completeness

Lp spaces are complete for the standard norm range. Norm-Cauchy approximation schemes therefore have limits inside the same space.

4.2 · Duality and approximation

Duality represents continuous linear functionals through integration. Density results reduce many proofs to simple functions.

4.2.1 · Dual spaces

A dual space consists of continuous linear functionals. Lp duality identifies many of these functionals with conjugate-exponent functions under the relevant measure assumptions.

4.2.2 · Dense simple functions

Simple functions are dense in Lp for finite p. Approximation by them lets elementary identities extend to arbitrary elements.

4.2.3 · Convergence in Lp

Lp convergence controls an integrated power of the error. It differs from pointwise convergence and must be tracked separately.

5 · Banach and Hilbert spaces

Completeness distinguishes Banach spaces from general normed spaces. Hilbert spaces combine completeness with inner-product geometry.

5.1 · Normed and complete spaces

Completeness distinguishes Banach spaces from general normed spaces. Bounded operators provide their natural continuous linear maps.

5.1.1 · Banach spaces

A Banach space is a normed vector space in which every Cauchy sequence converges. Completeness supports fixed-point and operator theorems.

5.1.2 · Bounded operators

A bounded linear operator satisfies a uniform norm estimate. For linear maps between normed spaces, boundedness is equivalent to continuity.

5.1.3 · Examples

Sequence spaces and spaces of continuous functions give standard Banach examples. Comparing them clarifies the effect of the chosen norm.

5.2 · Hilbert geometry

Hilbert spaces combine completeness with inner-product geometry. Orthogonality yields projections and representations unavailable in arbitrary Banach spaces.

5.2.1 · Orthogonality

Orthogonal vectors have zero inner product. This relation supports Pythagorean decompositions and least-squares reasoning.

5.2.2 · Projection theorem

Every vector has a unique closest point in a nonempty closed convex subset of a Hilbert space. For closed subspaces, the residual is orthogonal to the subspace.

5.2.3 · Riesz representation

The Hilbert-space Riesz theorem represents every continuous linear functional by an inner product with a unique vector. It converts duality into geometry.

6 · Linear operators and core theorems

Operator norms, adjoints, and inverses describe how linear maps act quantitatively. The fundamental theorems turn extension, completeness, and category arguments into operator results.

6.1 · Operator theory

Operator norms, adjoints, and inverses describe how linear maps act quantitatively. They supply the language for spectral and stability questions.

6.1.1 · Operator norms

The operator norm is the largest amplification of a unit vector. It controls continuity, compositions, and perturbations.

6.1.2 · Adjoints

Adjoints transfer an operator across a dual pairing or inner product. They connect range, kernel, and orthogonality properties.

6.1.3 · Inverse operators

An inverse operator undoes a bijective linear map. Boundedness of that inverse requires analytic structure beyond algebraic invertibility in general settings.

6.2 · Fundamental principles

The fundamental theorems turn extension, completeness, and category arguments into operator results. Each theorem requires its own precise setting.

6.2.1 · Hahn–Banach theorem

Hahn–Banach extends dominated linear functionals without increasing the controlling bound. It supplies separating functionals and dual-space richness.

6.2.2 · Uniform boundedness

Uniform boundedness upgrades pointwise bounds on a family of bounded linear maps to an operator-norm bound on a Banach domain. Baire category is its core mechanism.

6.2.3 · Open mapping and closed graph

The open mapping theorem controls surjective maps between Banach spaces, and the closed graph theorem controls closed-graph linear maps. Both reveal the force of completeness.

7 · Weak topologies and compactness

Weak topologies test vectors through linear functionals. Weak compactness can replace strong compactness in infinite-dimensional problems.

7.1 · Weak convergence

Weak topologies test vectors through linear functionals. They permit convergence in settings where norm convergence is too demanding.

7.1.1 · Weak and weak-star topologies

Weak convergence uses all continuous linear functionals, while weak-star convergence tests a dual space on its predual. These topologies retain different amounts of information.

7.1.2 · Nets and sequences

Nets extend sequences to directed index sets. They characterize topological closure and compactness without countability assumptions.

7.1.3 · Examples

Orthonormal sequences illustrate weak convergence without norm convergence. Such examples show why weak limits may preserve less geometry.

7.2 · Compactness principles

Weak compactness can replace strong compactness in infinite-dimensional problems. Duality and reflexivity identify settings where boundedness suffices.

7.2.1 · Banach–Alaoglu

Banach–Alaoglu makes the closed dual unit ball compact in the weak-star topology. Sequential conclusions need additional hypotheses.

7.2.2 · Reflexivity

A Banach space is reflexive when its canonical map to the bidual is surjective. Reflexivity characterizes weak compactness of the closed unit ball.

7.2.3 · Weak compactness

Weak compactness supplies convergent nets and, in Banach-space settings, suitable subsequence principles. It is central to variational existence arguments.

8 · Compact operators and spectral theory

Compact operators turn bounded sets into relatively compact sets. Spectral theory studies when an operator minus a scalar multiple of the identity is invertible.

8.1 · Compactness

Compact operators turn bounded sets into relatively compact sets. Their behavior resembles finite-dimensional linear algebra in important respects.

8.1.1 · Finite-rank approximation

Finite-rank maps have finite-dimensional ranges and are compact. Norm approximation by finite-rank maps is available in important settings, including Hilbert spaces, but needs care in general Banach spaces.

8.1.2 · Compact operators

Compact operators send bounded sequences to sequences with convergent image subsequences. This property provides stronger control than boundedness alone.

8.1.3 · Fredholm alternatives

Fredholm alternatives relate solvability of compact perturbations of the identity to their kernels and adjoints. They distinguish unique solvability from finite-dimensional obstructions.

8.2 · Spectral theory

Spectral theory studies when an operator minus a scalar multiple of the identity is invertible. Compactness and self-adjointness yield particularly explicit structure.

8.2.1 · Spectrum and resolvent

The spectrum consists of scalars where bounded invertibility fails. The resolvent records the inverse outside that set.

8.2.2 · Compact self-adjoint operators

Compact self-adjoint operators admit orthogonal eigenvector descriptions on Hilbert spaces. Their nonzero eigenvalues have finite multiplicity and can accumulate only at zero.

8.2.3 · Spectral theorem

The spectral theorem represents self-adjoint operators through spectral data in its appropriate version. The compact version gives eigen-expansions, while broader versions require spectral measures.

Part III · Further Theory and Applications

9 · Distributions and weak derivatives

Distributions extend functions by viewing them as linear actions on test functions. Weak derivatives lead to spaces adapted to partial differential equations.

9.1 · Test functions

Distributions extend functions by viewing them as linear actions on test functions. The test-function topology encodes the continuity needed for differentiation.

9.1.1 · Smooth compact support

Smooth compactly supported functions localize calculations while allowing arbitrary differentiation. They form the standard test-function space for distributions.

9.1.2 · Distributions

A distribution is a continuous linear functional on the test-function space. This includes ordinary locally integrable functions and singular objects such as point masses.

9.1.3 · Distributional derivatives

Distributional derivatives transfer differentiation to a test function with a sign change. This definition extends differentiation to nonsmooth objects.

9.2 · Function-space bridges

Weak derivatives lead to spaces adapted to partial differential equations. Duality then expresses equations even when classical derivatives do not exist.

9.2.1 · Sobolev spaces

Sobolev spaces require a function and specified weak derivatives to satisfy integrability conditions. They encode regularity in a form compatible with weak limits.

9.2.2 · Weak formulations

A weak formulation tests an equation against admissible functions and integrates by parts. It lowers differentiability requirements on the unknown.

9.2.3 · Duality in PDE

Dual spaces describe forcing terms and residuals in weak PDE formulations. This makes solvability a question about operators between function spaces.

10 · Advanced topics and applications

Banach algebras combine complete normed spaces with compatible multiplication. Further directions reuse the core language in several settings.

10.1 · Banach algebras

Banach algebras combine complete normed spaces with compatible multiplication. Their spectra extend matrix eigenvalue ideas to abstract algebraic settings.

10.1.1 · Spectrum and spectral radius

The spectral radius measures the largest modulus in the spectrum. The spectral-radius formula connects it to asymptotic growth of powers.

10.1.2 · Gelfand transform overview

For a commutative unital Banach algebra, the Gelfand transform evaluates elements on characters. It converts algebraic elements into functions on a spectral space.

10.2 · Selected directions

Further directions reuse the core language in several settings. Each route introduces additional structure beyond the common Banach-space framework.

10.2.1 · Operator algebras

Operator algebras study algebras of operators with norm or weak closure conditions. They connect spectral theory to noncommutative structures.

10.2.2 · Ergodic theory

Ergodic theory studies transformations preserving a measure. Functional analysis supplies operator methods for long-time averages.

10.2.3 · Locally convex spaces or vector-valued integration

Locally convex spaces generalize normed spaces through families of seminorms, while vector-valued integration extends scalar integration. These routes support distribution theory and evolution equations.
